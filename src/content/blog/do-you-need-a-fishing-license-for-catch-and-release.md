Every spring someone on r/texas or r/FishingForBeginners asks the same thing: if I throw them all back, do I still need a license? The short answer is yes. The longer answer is that there are legitimate ways to fish without one, and they have nothing to do with releasing the fish.

## Why catch and release still needs a license

State fishing laws are written around the act, not the outcome. Tennessee's agency puts it as plainly as anyone: if you take, or attempt to take, fish by any method, or help someone else do it, you need a valid license. Texas uses the same "take or attempt to take" language for its public waters. Once your line is in the water with a hook on it, you are fishing, whether the fish ends up in a cooler or back in the lake.

There is also a money reason the rule won't change. License sales and the federal excise tax on tackle, distributed to states partly in proportion to license holders, fund the hatcheries, surveys, boat ramps and fishing piers that catch-and-release anglers use as much as anyone. TWRA states that 100% of its license dollars go to wildlife conservation.

So if a warden walks up while you're releasing a bass, "I'm letting it go" is not a defense. The good news: the actual exemptions are generous if you know where to look.

## The exemptions that actually exist

These are the patterns you'll find state to state. The details differ, so read your state's rule, not a forum post.

- **Age.** Every state exempts young kids. The cutoff ranges from under 13 to under 17 among our five pilot states. Many states also exempt very old residents, but the threshold is usually a birth date long ago (Texas: born before January 1, 1931; Tennessee: born before March 1, 1926), so most seniors buy a discounted senior license instead.
- **Free fishing days.** Most states run one or more days or weekends a year when anyone can fish public water without a license. They cluster in early June. Bag and length limits still apply.
- **State parks.** Some states let you fish inside state parks without a license. Texas and Minnesota (residents) both do, with conditions.
- **Private ponds.** Water fully enclosed on private land is often exempt, but "connected to a creek" or "too big" can break the exemption.
- **Licensed piers.** In some coastal states, a pier can buy a license that covers everyone fishing from it. Florida does this for saltwater piers.
- **Military, disability and landowner exemptions.** These exist in many states but have paperwork requirements. Check the agency page.

What is **not** an exemption anywhere we checked: catch and release, fishing from the bank instead of a boat, "just helping," or only fishing for a few minutes.

### Texas

Texas Parks and Wildlife says residents and non-residents who fish public waters need a license, with exemptions for anyone under 17 and residents born before January 1, 1931. A license is really a package: you need the freshwater or saltwater endorsement for the water you fish. Resident packages are $30 freshwater, $35 saltwater and $40 all-water. Non-resident packages are $58, $63 and $68. A one-day all-water license is $11 for residents and $16 for non-residents. Annual packages run through August 31, not 12 months from purchase, so a license bought in July is a short one.

The biggest legal no-license option is state parks. TPWD says more than 70 state parks offer license-free fishing year round on park property and in waters fully enclosed by a park. You still pay park entry, and on piers you're limited to pole and line, two poles per person. Many of those parks also lend rods. Details are on our [Texas license page](/fishing/texas/fishing-license).

### Florida

Florida's freshwater rules (FWC regulations, published through eRegulations) exempt anyone under 16 and Florida residents 65 or older carrying proof of age and residency, such as a Florida driver license or the free Resident 65+ certificate. You also don't need a license on a man-made pond of 20 acres or less entirely on your own property with no surface connection to public waters.

Two Florida quirks catch people. First, licensing follows the species you're fishing for, not just where you stand, and freshwater and saltwater licenses are separate. A resident annual freshwater license is $17. Non-residents pay $47 for a year, $30 for seven days or $17 for three days. Second, saltwater anglers on a pier that holds a valid pier saltwater license are covered by the pier's license. Ask at the bait window whether the pier is licensed. More on our [Florida license page](/fishing/florida/fishing-license).

### Minnesota

Minnesota requires a license for residents 16 through 89. Residents under 16 and 90 or older are exempt. Residents can also fish without a license in state parks for any species except trout, when shore fishing or wading on park land, or fishing from a boat or the ice on water entirely inside the park.

Minnesota's license-free weekends are built around family: Take a Mom Fishing Weekend around Mother's Day, Take a Kid Fishing Weekend in early June, and Take a Kid Ice Fishing Weekend in January, when adults fishing with a child under 16 don't need a license. Exact dates move every year, so check the DNR or our [Minnesota license page](/fishing/minnesota/fishing-license) before you go.

### Michigan

Michigan requires a license at 17. Anglers under 17 can fish without one but must follow all rules. The interesting part is the parent rule. Any adult actively assisting a minor needs a license, but an unlicensed adult may still land the fish, unhook it, set up the rod, bait the hook, untangle line or cast for a young angler, as long as the child is the one fishing.

Michigan runs two Free Fishing Weekends each year, one in February and one in June. In 2026 they were February 14–15 and June 13–14. All fishing rules still apply. See our [Michigan license page](/fishing/michigan/fishing-license).

### Tennessee

Tennessee has the lowest youth cutoff of the five: anglers under 13 are exempt. Ages 13 to 15 buy a Junior Hunt, Fish and Trap license ($9 as of November 2026). For adults, TWRA sells a one-day no-trout fishing license for $6 (ages 13 to 64), a one-day all-species license for $11, and the annual Combination Hunt/Fish for $33 (ages 16 to 64). Trout need a $21 supplemental license on top.

Tennessee is also the strictest about helping: assisting someone else to take fish counts as fishing. Resident landowners and close family fishing their own farmland are exempt with TWRA's farmland form. The next Bobby Wilson Free Fishing Day is June 12, 2027, and kids 15 and under fish free during Free Fishing Week, June 12–18, 2027. Our [Tennessee license page](/fishing/tennessee/fishing-license) has the full fee table.

## Saltwater vs freshwater: the license people buy wrong

In coastal states, freshwater and saltwater are separate privileges, and anglers regularly buy the wrong one. One r/Fishing comment sums up the classic mix-up: "Ma'am are you aware you have a coastal fishing license." In Texas, you need the endorsement that matches the water. In Florida, you need the license for the species you're targeting. If you fish both, the all-water or combination option is usually cheaper than buying both separately.

Brackish water such as tidal rivers is where most mistakes happen. If you're unsure, check where the agency draws the line, or buy the combined license.

## Other states

The pattern holds outside our five pilot states: a license is required on public water whether you keep fish or not, with exemptions for youth, free fishing days and some private water. The age cutoff, senior rules and free-day dates are the parts that change. Our [state fishing hub](/fishing) links to every state wildlife agency, which is the only source worth trusting for your state's specifics.

## Can you still buy fishing licenses at Walmart?

In many states, yes. Walmart stores act as license agents at the sporting goods counter, but participation depends on the state and on the store, and some stores have scaled back that counter. Call ahead, or skip the drive:

- **Texas:** online at the TPWD license sales site, or at a retail license agent (TPWD's site has a retailer finder).
- **Florida:** online, through FWC's license system, at county tax collector offices, or at retail agents.
- **Minnesota and Michigan:** online through the DNR licensing system, the DNR app (Michigan), or a license agent.
- **Tennessee:** online through Go Outdoors Tennessee, or at TWRA agents including county clerks, sporting goods stores, hardware stores and boat docks.

Many states accept a digital license on your phone. Screenshot it or save the PDF, because agency apps don't always work without signal at the lake.

## The bottom line

If you're fishing public water and you're old enough to need a license, catch and release doesn't change anything. If you want to fish for free legally, use a free fishing day, a state park that allows it, or a private pond that qualifies. Buy the right license for the water, keep a copy on your phone, and spend the rest of your energy on the fish.
