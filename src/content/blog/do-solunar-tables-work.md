Every angler has seen it. The app says "excellent," the moon is full, you're on the water during the major period, and you get skunked. Then on a day the table rates "poor," you catch your PB. One r/bassfishing user put it this way: "Many of my best days are on the worst fishing days according to them."

That doesn't prove the tables are useless. It does mean the question deserves real data instead of anecdotes. There is real data, and it's more interesting than either side of the argument usually admits.

## What is the solunar fishing theory?

The theory comes from John Alden Knight, a fly fisherman and outdoor writer. In 1926 he went through fishing and hunting folklore and listed 33 factors that might affect when fish and game move. He threw out all but three: the sun, the moon and the tide. He named the idea "solunar," from the Latin for sun plus "lunar." He published the first solunar table in 1936 and expanded the idea in his 1942 book *Moon Up, Moon Down*.

Knight's tables mark four daily events:

- **Major periods:** the moon directly overhead, and the moon directly underfoot (on the other side of the Earth). Most tables show these as windows of about two hours.
- **Minor periods:** moonrise and moonset. Most tables show these as windows of about an hour.
- **Day rating:** stronger near new and full moons, when the sun and moon line up. Many tables add a boost when a period overlaps sunrise or sunset.

Publishers don't agree on exact window lengths, and different sites can give different times for the same place. Knight never explained how the moon would cause fish to feed. He pointed to "inland tides" and gravity, but never proposed a biological mechanism.

## What the studies actually found

Here are the most useful studies on moon effects in freshwater sport fishing, with their real numbers.

| Study | Fish and place | Data | What they found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vinson & Angradi, 2014 | Muskie, U.S. and Canada | 341,959 angler-reported catches, 1970–2013 | More fish caught around new and full moons. Fishing only on peak lunar days would catch about 5% more fish. Moon overhead or underfoot wasn't tested |
| Shaw, Renik & Sass, 2021 | Walleye and muskie, Escanaba Lake, Wisconsin | 2,005 walleye trips and 2,771 muskie trips, 2003–2015 | Moon position and phase had measurable effects, but guide use and live bait mattered most. Barometric pressure wasn't significant |
| Kuparinen et al., 2010 | Pike, a small lake in Germany | Experimental catch-and-release angling records | Catch rates higher around full and new moons, but recent fishing effort had a bigger effect, and so did water temperature, wind and dusk |
| Quigley et al., 2023 | Trout, North American freshwater | One experienced angler's trips, Sept 2013 to Feb 2019 | No relationship between catch rate and values from several solunar services, moon phase or moon brightness. Air temperature predicted catches better |
| Sullivan et al., 2016 | White-tailed deer, Southeastern U.S. | 38 GPS-collared bucks, 2010–2012 | Some link between deer activity and moon events, but the chart logic about moon phase "may be misleading" |

### The muskie study: a real effect, and a small one

The muskie study is the strongest evidence that the moon matters. It used a huge set of Muskies Inc. catch records and found that catches followed the 29-day lunar cycle. The overall effect was a maximum of about 5%. At night, the full moon effect was bigger. Fish over 40 inches showed a stronger pattern, and July and August showed more than June.

The authors were careful about the limits. The records were self-reported and had no effort data. Using creel surveys, they estimated a peak-day angler would catch each fish about 2 to 5 hours sooner. On Lake Vermilion, effort on peak days was 36% higher, so some of the "moon effect" may just be more people fishing. In the creel datasets they checked, catch per hour showed no lunar effect at all. An older look at about 67,000 of the same records, cited in the paper, found nothing different from random.

### The Wisconsin study: moon effects exist, but angler factors win

Escanaba Lake has a mandatory creel census, so every trip is recorded, including the ones that caught nothing. That's rare and valuable. Walleye trips were more successful with the moon underfoot and during gibbous phases (the days just before and after full). But walleye catch per hour was slightly lower with the moon overhead. Muskie trips were more successful with the moon overhead or underfoot, yet muskie catch per hour was lower at those times.

In other words, the moon factors pointed in different directions depending on how you measured success. The factors that pointed the same way every time were human ones: guided trips succeeded 91% of the time for walleye, against 60% for unguided trips.

### The trout study: no effect

The 2023 trout study tested the tables most directly. Values from several solunar services were strongly correlated with each other and with moon phase. None of them predicted catch rate. Air temperature did. The data came from one angler, which the authors acknowledge. But a single consistent angler is a decent test, because the skill level stays the same and only the conditions change.

## Why a real effect can still be useless on the water

Suppose the muskie result applies to your lake: 5% more fish on peak days. If you average two fish a trip, that's one extra fish every ten trips. A northwest wind after a front, a 6°F drop in water temperature, or a boat that hit your spot an hour earlier will swamp that.

That's the core problem with day ratings like "98% activity." They take a factor that explains a sliver of the variation and present it as the main event. Even fans of solunar apps say in their reviews that the table is only one factor among many. Water temperature, light and weather change are bigger levers. ## Why so many anglers swear by them

- **More fishing on "good" days.** If you go out more on days the table likes, you'll catch more fish on those days, even if the table does nothing. The Lake Vermilion data shows anglers do this.
- **Memorable fish stick.** A trophy caught during a major period gets told for years. A dead major period is forgotten by dinner.
- **Moon and light do matter at night.** A bright full moon over a clear lake changes how much light a hunting walleye has. Walleye feed most under low light, according to the federal habitat report on the species. So some of what anglers notice may be light, not "solunar" timing.
- **Major periods often overlap dawn and dusk.** When they do, the low-light bite gets the credit for both.

## What moon phase is bad for fishing?

No phase is reliably bad. Tables often rate the first and last quarter moons lowest, but none of the studies above found a dependable slump at the quarters. Where an effect showed up, it was a slight lift around new and full moons for muskie and pike, and around the gibbous days for Escanaba walleye.

If you fish clear water at night, a bright full moon is worth planning around. Try the darker hours before moonrise or after moonset, or move to stained water. That's not solunar theory. It's just light.

## How to test solunar tables yourself

You don't need a 341,000-fish dataset. You need honest records and some patience.

1. **Fish on a schedule, not on ratings.** If you only go on "good" days, you can't compare. Fish your usual days and let the moon fall where it falls.
2. **Write down every trip, including the zeros.** Start time, end time, fish caught. Skunked trips are the most important rows.
3. **Note the solunar data before you look at results.** Moon phase, and whether any part of your trip overlapped a major or minor period.
4. **Record the bigger factors too.** Water temperature, sky, wind direction and speed, and the pressure trend. Otherwise the moon gets credit for a front.
5. **Compare fish per hour,** inside the windows against outside them. Don't compare fish per trip.
6. **Wait for 30 or more trips** before deciding anything. Ten trips can tell you whatever you want to hear.

A simple log looks like this:

| Date | Hours | Fish | Fish/hr | Moon phase | In a major or minor? | Water °F | Sky | Wind | Pressure trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr 12 | 3.0 | 4 | 1.3 | Waxing gibbous | Major, last hour | 61 | Overcast | S 8 mph | Falling |
| Apr 19 | 2.5 | 0 | 0 | Last quarter | No | 57 | Clear | NW 15 mph | Rising |

## So, should you use solunar tables?

Use them as a tiebreaker. If Saturday and Sunday look the same on water temperature, light and weather, take the day nearer the new or full moon and plan around a major period that lines up with dawn or dusk. Don't cancel a trip because the table says "poor," and don't expect a "best day" to save you from a cold front.
