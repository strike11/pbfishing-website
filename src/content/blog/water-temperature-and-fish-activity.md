If you only track one condition, make it water temperature. Fish are cold-blooded. Their body runs at the temperature of the water around them, so the water decides how fast they digest, how far they'll chase a lure, where they sit, and when they spawn. Weather, pressure and the moon all matter less. Our [best time to fish guide](/blog/best-time-to-fish) ranks them.

Below are the numbers for seven popular freshwater species, then how lakes layer and turn over, and how to measure water temperature yourself. The figures come from U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service habitat reports and state agencies. We converted Celsius figures to Fahrenheit and rounded them.

| Smallmouth bass | Males move up near 60. Usually 62–64, seen as low as 53 | — | — | Prefers cool, clear water with rock and gravel |

| Walleye | Starts about 45–48, most at 43–52. Minnesota DNR: peak 42–50 | 68–75 | Growth stops below 54. Avoids water above 75. Upper lethal range 84–90 | Spawns at night on rock and gravel |

Read these as ranges, not switches. Fish in a shallow, dark-bottomed bay can spawn a week before fish in the main lake. A warm spell can pull fish shallow early, and a cold front can push them back. Day length matters too, especially for the spawn.

## Species notes

### Largemouth bass

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service habitat report says largemouth spawning usually starts when water reaches 12–15.5°C (about 54–60°F), with most spawning between 61 and 72°F. Adults grow best at 75–86°F, and grow very little below 59°F. That's why the spring bite in the South starts so much earlier than up north. Texas Parks and Wildlife says spawning can start anywhere from February to May depending on where you are in the state. Florida-strain bass nest earlier and at slightly lower temperatures than northern bass. See the [largemouth bass guide](/species/largemouth-bass) and the bass pages for [Texas](/fishing/texas/bass-fishing), [Florida](/fishing/florida/bass-fishing) and [Tennessee](/fishing/tennessee/bass-fishing).

### Smallmouth bass

The Wisconsin DNR says smallmouth usually spawn at 62–64°F but have been found spawning at 53°F. In southern Wisconsin, the spawn runs from mid-May through June. Texas Parks and Wildlife says males move into spawning areas as water nears 60°F. Their eggs take about 10 days to hatch in the mid-50s, but only 2–3 days in the mid-70s. Smallmouth like cooler, clearer, rockier water than largemouth. See the [Michigan](/fishing/michigan/bass-fishing) and [Minnesota](/fishing/minnesota/bass-fishing) bass pages.

### Crappie

Texas Parks and Wildlife says white crappie nest when water reaches 65–70°F, and spawning has been seen as low as 56°F. Crappie stage near brush and channel edges in the high 50s, then pile into shallow cover. That makes the spring spawn the easiest time of year to fill a bucket. See the [crappie guide](/species/crappie).

### Bluegill

Texas Parks and Wildlife says bluegill start spawning when water reaches about 70°F, usually peaking in May or June, and keep spawning until the water cools in fall. Nests sit in 1–2 feet of water. Because they spawn over such a long season, you'll find bedding bluegill on warm shallow flats all summer. See the [bluegill guide](/species/bluegill).

### Channel catfish

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service says channel catfish spawn at 72–73°F. The Missouri Department of Conservation puts it at 75°F, around late May in Missouri. Adults rest in deep water or around logs by day and move shallow to feed at night. Summer nights in the upper 70s and 80s are the classic catfish window. See the [channel catfish guide](/species/channel-catfish).

### Walleye

The federal walleye report says spawning usually starts at 7–9°C (about 45–48°F), with most of it at 43–52°F, on rock and gravel, at night. Minnesota DNR puts the peak at 42–50°F. Adults grow best at 68–75°F, try to avoid water warmer than 75°F, and stop growing below about 54°F. In summer, that pushes walleye to deeper, cooler water during the day. See the [walleye guide](/species/walleye) and [Minnesota's seasons calendar](/fishing/minnesota/fish-species-seasons).

### Rainbow trout

The federal rainbow trout report puts the best range at 54–64°F. Lake rainbows avoid water above 64°F, growth stops at about 73°F, and 77°F is treated as the upper limit, survivable only briefly. Montana closes rivers to afternoon fishing (2 p.m. to midnight) when the water hits 73°F for three days in a row, and at 66°F for three days in cutthroat trout waters. Rainbows spawn almost entirely in streams, so lakes without inlet or outlet streams generally don't hold a reproducing population. In much of the South, they're stocked only in winter, because summer water gets too warm for them. See the [rainbow trout guide](/species/rainbow-trout).

## Turnover and the thermocline

Water temperature doesn't just change through the year. In deeper lakes and ponds, it also changes with depth, and that controls where fish can live.

**Summer layers.** The Missouri Department of Conservation explains that in summer, many ponds and lakes split into three layers:

- **The epilimnion,** the warm top layer. It stays mixed and full of oxygen from wind and plants. In summer, almost all fish live here.
- **The thermocline,** the middle layer, where temperature drops at least 0.5°F for every foot of depth.
- **The hypolimnion,** the cold bottom layer. It's cut off from the air, and rotting plants can use up its oxygen. By mid-July it may have none at all.

That's why "go deep to find cool water" doesn't always work. In many lakes, the deep cold water is empty of fish because it has no oxygen. Summer fish often sit near the top of the thermocline, where water is cooler but still has oxygen. Your sonar often shows the thermocline as a fuzzy band, with fish and bait right above it.

**Fall turnover.** As the surface cools, it becomes denser than the water below it and sinks. Wind finishes the job, and the whole lake mixes, sometimes literally overnight. Turnover brings oxygen to all depths, but the mixing can stir up odor and color, and fish scatter. For a few days afterward, fishing can be strange. Try rivers, creeks, shallow flats, or a different lake that has already turned over.

**Winter and spring.** Water is densest at 39°F. That's why ice floats and why fish can survive winter under it, in water around 39°F at the bottom. In spring, the surface warms to 39°F, sinks, and the lake mixes again. After that, fish can be found at any depth.

## Surface temperature vs. the depth you're fishing

The temperature on your sonar screen is usually the top foot or so of water. On a sunny spring afternoon, it can read several degrees warmer than the water 10 feet down. On a cold morning after a front, it can read colder. Neither tells you what a fish on a 15-foot ledge is feeling.

Some rules of thumb:

- **Note the trend, not just today's number.** Three days of warming at 56°F fishes very differently from a sudden drop from 62°F to 56°F.
- **Find the warmest water in early spring.** Shallow, dark-bottomed bays and north-shore pockets that get the afternoon sun warm first.
- **Find the coolest water with oxygen in summer.** That means shade, current, springs, inflows and the top of the thermocline.
- **Compare spots.** A 3°F difference between two creek arms in March can mean fish in one and nothing in the other.

## How to measure water temperature

- **Sonar or fish finder.** Most have a surface temperature sensor. It's great for comparing spots and watching the trend through the day, but it only reads the surface.
- **A thermometer on a string.** Lower a fishing thermometer to the depth you're fishing, leave it for a minute or two, and read it fast after you pull it up. A cheap aquarium thermometer works in a pinch.
- **A stream thermometer for trout.** Check the water in the afternoon on hot days. If it's in the low 70s, call it a day for trout.
- **River gauges.** Many U.S. Geological Survey stream gauges report water temperature online in real time. That's handy for planning river trips before you leave.
- **Your own log.** Write the temperature, depth and time next to every fish. After a season, you'll know the numbers for your own water.
