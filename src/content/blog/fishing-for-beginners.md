Most first trips go wrong in the same three places: too much gear, the wrong water, and a target fish that is hard to catch. Fix those and your odds of a bent rod on day one go way up. Everything below is aimed at a bank angler with no boat and no fishing friends, which, judging by r/FishingForBeginners, is most of you.

## Step zero: the license

Get this out of the way first. In every state we cover, adults need a license to fish public water, and **releasing the fish does not change that**. The rules key on the act of fishing, not on keeping anything. Youth exemptions vary a lot: under 17 in Texas and Michigan, under 16 in Florida and for Minnesota residents, under 13 in Tennessee. Our explainer on [whether you need a license for catch and release](/blog/do-you-need-a-fishing-license-for-catch-and-release) covers free fishing days, private ponds and piers, and each state has its own page, for example [Texas](/fishing/texas/fishing-license) and [Florida](/fishing/florida/fishing-license).

Two money-savers worth knowing. Texas lets anyone fish without a license from the bank or a pier in more than 70 state parks (you still pay park entry). And most states run one or more free fishing days a year, usually in early June.

## The first-trip checklist

You can do all of this for about $100. Spend most of it on the rod and reel and almost nothing on lures.

| Item | What to buy | Why | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rod and reel | 6 ft 6 in to 7 ft, medium-light, spinning combo | Casts light baits, forgiving, works for panfish to 5 lb bass | $40 to $80 |
| Line | 6 to 8 lb monofilament (often pre-spooled) | Stretches, floats, easy knots | $5 to $10 |
| Hooks | Size 6 and 8 long-shank (Aberdeen) for panfish; a few size 1/0 to 2/0 for catfish | Small hooks catch more fish than big ones | $5 |
| Bobbers | Three 1-inch round clip-on bobbers, one slip bobber | Holds bait at a depth and shows the bite | $5 |
| Sinkers | Assorted split shot (BB and #7), a few 1/4 to 1/2 oz egg sinkers | Gets bait down; egg sinkers for bottom rigs | $5 |
| Live bait | A dozen nightcrawlers or red wigglers, or crickets | Everything eats a worm | $4 to $6 |
| Tools | Needle-nose pliers, nail clipper for line | Unhooking and cutting | $10 |
| Extras | Small tackle box, towel, polarized sunglasses, hat, sunscreen, water | Comfort and seeing into the water | Varies |

What you can skip: a vest, a tackle bag with 300 lures, a fish finder, and anything labeled "fish attractant." Buy two or three lures at most, such as a 1/16 oz inline spinner and a pack of 4-inch soft plastic worms, so you have something to try when you run out of bait.

<p class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Have the shop spool your reel. Line twisted onto a spinning reel the wrong way causes most of the bird's nests that end beginner trips early.</p>

## What's the difference between fishing lines?

This is one of the most-asked beginner questions, and the answer is simpler than the tackle wall makes it look.

| Line | Stretch | Visibility | Floats? | Best for | Beginner verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monofilament | High | Low to medium | Yes | Bobber rigs, topwater, general use | Start here |
| Fluorocarbon | Low to medium | Lowest | Sinks | Clear water, leaders, bottom baits | Second spool later |
| Braid | Almost none | High | Yes | Heavy cover, frogs, long casts | Wait a season |

Mono's stretch is a feature for a beginner: it cushions a late, hard hookset and a fish thrashing at your feet. Braid is wonderful in thick grass but it digs into itself on the spool, slips with some knots and shows up like a rope in clear water. If you do use braid, tie a 3 to 6 foot fluorocarbon leader.

## Where to go on your first trip

Pick water that is small, public and stocked. In rough order of how easy they are:

1. **Community fishing ponds and park lakes.** Many cities and state agencies stock small ponds with catfish, sunfish and bass specifically for new anglers. Tennessee's agency, for example, runs a list of Family Fishing Lakes open year round. Our state beginner pages list spots by city: [Texas](/fishing/texas/beginner-fishing-spots), [Florida](/fishing/florida/beginner-fishing-spots), [Minnesota](/fishing/minnesota/beginner-fishing-spots), [Michigan](/fishing/michigan/beginner-fishing-spots) and [Tennessee](/fishing/tennessee/beginner-fishing-spots).
2. **State parks.** Restrooms, parking, clear rules and often a fishing pier. Many Texas state parks lend rods and tackle for use in the park, which means your first trip can cost only the entry fee.
3. **Public piers and docks.** A pier puts you over deeper water without a boat. Fish the shade under it and the corners.
4. **Rivers below dams and spillways.** Lots of fish, but fast water and slick rocks. Save this for trip three.

Avoid anything posted, anything you would have to cross private land to reach, and big open reservoir banks with no structure. For how to scout water on your own, see [how to find fishing spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots).

## What to catch first

Target **bluegill and other sunfish**. They live in almost every pond in the country, bite in daylight, hang near the bank, and fight hard for their size. A morning of 20 bluegill teaches you more about bites, hooksets and depth than a morning of waiting on one bass. Our [bluegill guide](/species/bluegill) covers where they hold by season.

Second choice: **channel catfish** in stocked ponds, on a bottom rig with nightcrawlers or chicken liver in the evening. Third: **largemouth bass**, which are more about lures and patience. When you are ready, the [largemouth bass guide](/species/largemouth-bass) is the next read.

## Two simple rigs

### The bobber rig (panfish, crappie, small bass)

1. Tie a size 6 or 8 hook to the end of your line.
2. Pinch one or two small split shot onto the line 6 to 12 inches above the hook.
3. Clip a bobber on 2 to 4 feet above the hook. Start shallow near the bank, go deeper if nothing bites in 10 minutes.
4. Thread a piece of nightcrawler about an inch long onto the hook, covering the point but leaving a little tail wiggling.

Cast near something: a dock post, the edge of weeds, a fallen tree. When the bobber dips under or slides sideways, reel up the slack and lift the rod firmly. Don't yank. Panfish have small mouths, so if you keep missing, use a smaller hook and a smaller piece of bait.

### The bottom rig (catfish)

1. Slide a 1/4 to 1/2 oz egg sinker onto the main line.
2. Tie on a small barrel swivel so the sinker can't slide down.
3. Tie 12 to 18 inches of line to the other end of the swivel, then a 1/0 or 2/0 hook.
4. Bait with a whole nightcrawler or a chunk of cut bait, cast out, and let it sit on the bottom with the line just tight.

The sliding sinker lets a catfish pick up the bait without feeling weight. When the rod tip loads up and stays loaded, reel down and set the hook.

If you want to try a bass lure, rig a 5-inch soft plastic worm Texas-style on a 3/0 offset hook with a 1/8 oz bullet weight and drag it slowly along the bottom. It is weedless and catches bass almost everywhere.

## Two knots that cover everything

### Improved clinch knot

1. Pass the line through the hook eye and pull 6 inches through.
2. Wrap the tag end around the main line 5 to 7 times.
3. Push the tag end through the small loop just above the eye.
4. Then tuck it back through the big loop you just made.
5. Wet the knot with spit or water and pull it tight slowly. Trim the tag to about 1/8 inch.

Use 7 wraps on light line, 5 on heavier line. It is the standard knot for monofilament but slips on braid.

### Palomar knot

1. Double 6 inches of line into a loop and push the loop through the hook eye.
2. Tie a loose overhand knot with the doubled line, leaving the hook hanging in the loop.
3. Pass the loop over the entire hook.
4. Wet it and pull both the main line and tag end to tighten. Trim.

The Palomar is one of the strongest simple knots and works with mono, fluorocarbon and braid. Its only drawback is passing the loop over a big lure with several treble hooks.

Practice both at the kitchen table with a hook and old line. Tying knots for the first time with cold hands and a fish breaking water behind you is how gear gets lost.

## How to release a fish properly

Most fish you catch as a beginner should go back. The Keep Fish Wet principles boil it down to three things: minimize air exposure, avoid dry surfaces, and reduce handling time. In practice:

- Wet your hands before you touch the fish, and never lay it on hot concrete, a dry dock or sand.
- Hold bass by the lower lip and support the belly on anything over a couple of pounds. Never grab a fish by the gills.
- Use pliers to back the hook out the way it went in. If a fish swallowed the hook deep, cut the line close to the mouth rather than ripping it out.
- If you want a photo, have the camera ready before you lift the fish, and keep air time to a few seconds.
- Hold the fish upright in the water until it kicks away on its own.

## Etiquette nobody tells you

- **Give space.** On a bank or pier, leave at least a long cast's width between you and the next angler. If someone was there first, ask before setting up close.
- **Don't cast across other lines.** Cast straight out from where you stand.
- **Pack out line and bait containers.** Loose monofilament kills birds and turtles. Many public lakes have line recycling tubes; use them.
- **Don't share someone else's spot online.** If a regular shows you a good corner, keep it to yourself. Spot burning is one of the biggest complaints among anglers.
- **Know the limits** for anything you keep, including length limits. Check the state regulations, not a forum.

## Safety basics

- Wear a life jacket on any boat or kayak, and put one on kids near deep water, piers and riverbanks.
- Wear sunglasses. A flying hook or sinker is the most common fishing injury and eyes are the worst place to take one.
- Leave the water at the first sound of thunder. A graphite rod is a lightning rod.
- Ice fishing: don't walk on ice until you have checked your state agency's ice thickness guidance.
- If you plan to eat your catch, check your state's fish consumption advisory first.

## A realistic first day

Get there an hour after sunrise. Fish a bobber rig with worms near the first piece of cover you see. If 15 minutes pass with nothing, move 50 feet or change depth by a foot. Most beginners who come home skunked never moved or never changed anything. If it still happens, our list of [reasons you're not catching fish](/blog/why-am-i-not-catching-fish) goes through the usual fixes, one by one.

Tight lines, and go small. The big ones come after you've learned on the little ones.
