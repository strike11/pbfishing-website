Releasing a fish well is mostly about three things you control: how long you fight it, how long it's out of the water, and how you hold it. Get those right and most fish swim off fine. Get them wrong and a fish that looks healthy at release can die hours or days later. Here's what the research and the agencies say, in the order you'll need it on the water.

## Before you cast

Most bad releases start before the bite.

- **Have your tools within reach:** long-nose pliers or hemostats, line cutters, and a rubber or knotless net. Texas 4-H handling guidance recommends a rubber-mesh net over abrasive nylon.
- **Consider barbless hooks,** or pinch the barbs down. Research summarized by Trout Unlimited found barbed hooks took longer to remove, which means more air time.
- **Set the hook quickly** with bait so the fish doesn't swallow it.
- **Set up the photo now.** Camera unlocked, measuring board wet and in reach, buddy briefed. More on this below.

## Land it fast

A fish played to exhaustion is in worse shape before it ever leaves the water. Trout Unlimited's summary of catch-and-release studies notes that stress hormones in rainbow trout rose after just 2 to 3 minutes of being angled and handled, and kept rising. Use tackle heavy enough to land the fish you're targeting without a long fight. Ultralight gear on big fish is fun, but it's hard on them.

## Unhook it in the water if you can

The best release never takes the fish out of the water. Leave it in the net or hold it at the surface while you back the hook out the way it went in. Texas 4-H guidance adds a few practical tips:

- Use pliers, hemostats or a hook remover.
- Turning a fish on its back and covering its eyes can calm it.
- Don't jerk a leader to break it; that can injure organs.

## Handle it with wet hands

Fish are covered in a slime layer that protects them from parasites and infections. Dry hands, towels, boat carpet and hot decks strip it off.

- **Wet your hands** (or use a wet glove) before you touch the fish.
- **Keep it off the ground and the boat floor.**
- **Fingers out of the gills and eyes.** No "gill grabbing."
- **Support the belly.** Texas 4-H guidance says fish under about 5 pounds can be held vertically by the lower jaw; bigger fish should be held horizontally with two hands. A heavy bass or catfish hanging by its jaw is carrying its whole weight on that jaw.
- **Go easy with lip-grippers.** They protect your hands and the slime, but Trout Unlimited's summary cites research where mechanical lip-grippers damaged mouth tissue in 80 to 100% of fish of one species (bonefish). Use them gently and don't hang a big fish vertically from one.

## Keep air time short

This is the single biggest thing you control.

The study everyone cites is from Queen's University (Ferguson and Tufts, 1992). Rainbow trout that were exercised to simulate a fight had 88% survival. With **30 seconds in air** after exercise, survival fell to **62%**. With **60 seconds**, it fell to **28%**. Those were hatchery fish in a lab, and other studies have found smaller effects, so treat the exact numbers with care. The direction isn't in doubt.

A later study on brook trout (Schreer and others, 2005) found swimming performance held up with 60 seconds or less of air exposure but dropped 75% at 120 seconds, which leaves a released fish easier prey.

The guideline researchers landed on, as summarized by Trout Unlimited: **keep air exposure to 15 to 20 seconds**. A handy test from the Texas 4-H guidance: no longer than you can hold your breath.

<p class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> If you need more time, for a measurement or a second photo, put the fish back in the water between steps. Ten seconds out, back in to breathe, then ten more.</p>

## Get the photo first, and fast

Anglers want the photo, and that's fine. The trick is doing it without adding a minute of air time.

1. **Set up before the fish comes out.** Phone unlocked with the camera open, board wet and ready.
2. **Measure and shoot in one move.** Fish on the board, mouth closed, tail pinched, one photo from straight above. That's the shot that proves length. See [how to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish).
3. **Then the hero shot,** with the fish held horizontally and low over the water, belly supported.
4. **Back in the water.** Weight estimates can be done later from length.

## Deep-hooked fish: cut the line

If the hook is deep in the throat or gut, don't dig for it. A 2009 study on deeply hooked bluegill (Fobert and others) compared pulling the hook with cutting the line:

| Treatment | Died within 48 hours | Died within 10 days |
|---|---|---|
| Hooked shallow (control) | 0% | 4% |
| Deep-hooked, line cut | 8% | 12.5% |
| Deep-hooked, hook removed | 33% | 44% |

Within 10 days, 71.4% of the line-cut fish had expelled the hook on their own. Cut the line as close to the hook as you can and let the fish go. If deep hooking happens a lot, you're probably letting bait sit too long before setting the hook.

## Fish from deep water: barotrauma

When a fish comes up fast from deep water, the gas in its swim bladder expands. NOAA calls this barotrauma: a pressure-related injury caused by reeling a fish from depth. Signs include bulging eyes and a bloated belly, sometimes with the stomach pushed into the mouth. A fish with barotrauma often can't swim back down.

**In fresh water,** Minnesota DNR's advice is direct: if you fish deeper than 30 feet, the ethical choice is to keep the fish you catch and count them toward your daily limit, unless a slot limit requires release. Catch-and-release anglers should fish shallower. That matters for anyone chasing walleye, perch or crappie over deep basins, including through the ice.

**In salt water,** use a descending device: a weighted clip or box that takes the fish back down to depth, where the pressure shrinks the gas and the fish can swim off. NOAA's Return 'Em Right program supports this for Gulf reef fish. Under the federal DESCEND Act, anglers fishing for reef fish in Gulf federal waters had to carry a venting tool or descending device rigged and ready from January 13, 2022; NOAA's bulletin set that requirement to expire on January 13, 2026, so check current federal and state rules before you go. The bulletin defined a descending device as having at least a 16-ounce weight and 60 feet of line.

Don't vent (puncture) a fish unless you've been taught how and it's legal where you fish.

<p class="callout warn"><strong>Check first:</strong> Some fish must be released by law even if they look rough, because of a slot or a closed season. Know your state's rules for the species before you decide to keep a fish you'd otherwise release.</p>

## Hot water: release survival drops

Warm water holds less oxygen, and fish caught in it recover more slowly. Trout are the clearest case. Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks applies "hoot owl" restrictions on many rivers once water temperatures reach 73°F or higher for three consecutive days, closing fishing from 2 p.m. to midnight. A Montana State University study cited by FWP found trout caught and released from warmer water were far more likely to die than those taken from cooler water.

What to do in summer:

- **Fish early,** when water is coolest.
- **Shorten everything:** the fight, the handling, the photo.
- **Skip the trout stream** on the hottest afternoons, even where it's open.
- **For bass in hot weather,** keep livewell water fresh and cool and get tournament fish back quickly. Researchers recommend avoiding fishing during temperature extremes when you plan to release.

## Letting it go

- **Hold the fish upright in the water,** supporting its belly, facing into any current.
- **Let it swim off on its own.** If it rolls or sinks, recover it and try again. Texas 4-H guidance suggests gently moving it forward or facing it into the current to push water over the gills.
- **Watch it go.** A fish that swims down strongly is a good release.

## The 60-second checklist

| Step | Target |
|---|---|
| Fight | As short as your tackle allows |
| Unhook | In the water, pliers ready |
| Deep hook | Cut the line |
| Air time | 15–20 seconds, back in the water between steps |
| Hold | Wet hands, belly supported, horizontal for big fish |
| Photo | Board shot first, then the hero shot |
| Deep or hot water | Fish shallower or cooler if you plan to release, or keep what's legal |

None of this is complicated. It's just doing it in the right order, every time, so the fish you photograph today is still there to break someone's [personal best](/blog/how-to-catch-a-personal-best-bass) next year.
