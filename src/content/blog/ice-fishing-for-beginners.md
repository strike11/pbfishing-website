Ice fishing looks simple: drill a hole, drop a line, wait. Most first trips still end early for one of three reasons: the angler got cold, sat over empty water, or had no idea whether the ice would hold. Fix those and the rest is just fishing. This guide assumes you have no shelter, no electronics and no ice-fishing friends, and that you are walking out from a public access.

## Step one: ice you have checked yourself

Nobody certifies lake ice. The Minnesota DNR's ice poster leads with the warning that ice is never 100% safe, and the Michigan DNR says there is no reliable inch-thickness that makes ice safe. Treat every number below as a minimum, not a promise.

For new, clear ice, the Minnesota DNR's guideline is:

- **Under 4 inches:** keep off.
- **4 inches:** on foot, which covers walking out and fishing.
- **5 to 7 inches:** snowmobile.
- **7 to 8 inches:** side-by-side ATV.

White, snowy or slushy ice is weaker, so double those numbers. Ice also varies across the same lake: it is thinner near inlets, outlets, current, pressure ridges, docks with bubblers and anything sticking up through it like logs or brush. Our [ice thickness and safety guide](/blog/ice-thickness-safety) has the full table from several agencies, how to check ice with a spud bar and what to do if you go through.

For a first trip, the practical rules are short. Go after the locals are already out. Ask at the bait shop how much ice they have seen and where the bad spots are. Walk with a spud bar and hit the ice ahead of you every few steps. Wear ice picks around your neck, and do not go alone.

<p class="callout warn"><strong>Check first:</strong> Early ice and late ice are the riskiest weeks. If the bait shop says "people are out," ask how thick and where, then drill and measure your own hole before you settle in.</p>

## What to target first

Pick a fish that bites in winter, lives in water you can reach on foot and does not need a lot of gear. That means panfish.

| Fish | Where to look in winter | Typical depth | Best time | Bait |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Bluegill](/species/bluegill) and other sunfish | Weed edges, inside turns of weedlines, soft-bottom flats | 6 to 20 ft | Midday is fine | Wax worm or spikes on a 1/32 oz jig |
| Yellow perch | Hard-bottom flats, sand and gravel transitions, basin edges | 10 to 30 ft | Daylight | Spikes, minnow head, small spoon |
| [Crappie](/species/crappie) | Suspended over basins and deep weed edges, often off the bottom | 12 to 30 ft | Dusk and the first hour of dark | Small minnow or plastic on a 1/32 to 1/16 oz jig |
| [Walleye](/species/walleye) | Points, rock humps, drop-offs into deep water | 12 to 30 ft | Low light: dawn, dusk, cloudy days | Minnow on a tip-up, or a jigging spoon tipped with a minnow head |

Bluegill and perch are the best first fish. They travel in schools, so one bite usually means more, and they keep biting through the middle of the day when walleye go quiet. Crappie are a good second target once you learn to watch the middle of the water column, not just the bottom.

Save walleye for your second or third trip. They bite best in short low-light windows and relate to structure you need a map to find. In Minnesota, walleye season on inland waters runs through February 28, 2027 under the 2026 regulations. Check special regulations for your lake before you keep any; some big walleye lakes have tighter limits than the statewide rules.

## The minimum gear for a first trip

You do not need a shelter, a sled full of tackle or a $500 flasher. You need to make a hole, keep it open, fish it and get home safe.

- **License** (see below).
- **Hole maker:** a 6-inch hand auger is enough for panfish; go to 8 inches if you plan to set tip-ups for walleye or pike. A **spud bar** works too on thinner ice and doubles as your ice tester.
- **Skimmer:** a slotted scoop to clear slush and ice chips. Holes skim over fast when it is cold.
- **Ice rod:** a 24- to 30-inch light or ultralight rod with a small spinning reel and 2 to 4 lb line.
- **Terminal tackle:** a few 1/32 to 1/16 oz jigs in glow and natural colors, a couple of small spoons, a handful of split shot.
- **Bait:** wax worms or spikes for panfish, a dozen fathead minnows or shiners if you want to try a tip-up.
- **Bucket:** carries the gear, then becomes your seat.
- **Safety:** ice picks worn around your neck, a life jacket or float suit, ice cleats, a phone in a waterproof bag, a 50-foot throw rope.
- **Clothes:** layered, waterproof and windproof, with spare gloves.

The [ice fishing gear checklist](/blog/ice-fishing-gear-checklist) breaks this into a budget kit and a nice-to-have kit with approximate prices.

## Auger or spud bar?

A **spud bar** is a long steel shaft with a chisel end. It is cheap, never fails to start and is the best tool you own for testing ice as you walk. The downside is effort: chopping through a foot of ice takes a while and soaks your base layer.

A **hand auger** is a corkscrew blade you crank by hand. A sharp 6-inch hand auger goes through early-season ice quickly and is the usual beginner choice. Power augers (gas, propane or battery) save a lot of work once you drill a dozen holes a day, but they are a second-season purchase.

Most experienced anglers carry both early in the season: the spud for testing the path, the auger for fishing holes.

## Finding fish under the ice

Winter fish stack up on specific spots. Miss them and you can sit all day without a bite.

**Start with depth and structure.** Before you go, mark three spots on a lake map: a weed edge in 8 to 15 feet for bluegill, a flat or basin edge in 15 to 25 feet for perch, and a point or drop-off for walleye. Then go there. A cluster of other anglers is also a clue, but give them room (more on etiquette below).

**Drill a lot of holes.** The biggest difference between beginners and regulars is mobility. Drill a line of four to six holes across a depth change, fish each one for 10 to 15 minutes, and move on if nothing shows. Fish come to you less in winter. You go to them.

**Flasher basics.** A flasher is a sonar that shows what is under your hole in real time, as colored marks on a dial. You will see three things: the bottom (a wide band at the depth reading), your jig (a thin line that moves when you lift the rod) and fish (marks that rise toward or sit near your jig). On most units, stronger returns show in red, weaker ones in green or yellow. The magic of a flasher is that you can see a fish rise to your bait and stop it, then react: slow down, shake the jig in place, or lift it away to trigger a bite. You do not need one on day one, but it is the most useful upgrade you can buy after the basics.

Without a flasher, fish the bottom first. Lower your jig until it touches, then reel up a crank or two. Every few minutes, work up the water column a foot at a time, especially for crappie.

## Jigging vs tip-ups

There are two ways to fish through the ice, and the Michigan DNR describes both: hook-and-line jigging with short, limber rods, and tip-ups that hold a baited line under a flag that pops when a fish takes it.

**Jigging** is active. You hold the rod, lift and drop a small jig or spoon, and pause. Most bites come on the pause. For panfish, use tiny movements: a few quick shakes, then dead still for five seconds. Watch the rod tip and the line; winter bites are often a slight upward "lift" rather than a pull. Set the hook with a short wrist snap, not a full sweep.

**Tip-ups** are passive. You set a live minnow a foot or two off the bottom, flag cocked, and watch from a distance. When the flag goes up, walk (do not run) over, watch which way the spool is turning, let the fish run a few seconds for a pike or a beat longer for a walleye, then set the hook by hand and pull line in hand over hand.

Most anglers do both: one jigging rod in hand, tip-ups set nearby for bigger fish. Know your state's line limit before you set them:

- **Minnesota:** you may use **2 lines through the ice** (1 on designated trout lakes and streams), you must stay within sight of your lines, and you must be **within 200 feet of a tip-up**. New for 2026, non-motorized spring or bent-rod hook-setting devices are legal through the ice.
- **Michigan:** line limits, tip-up marking and shanty rules are in the 2026 Michigan Fishing Regulations, in effect until March 31, 2027. Read the ice section of the digest before you set more than one line.

## Staying warm

Cold ends more first trips than bad fishing. The Michigan DNR's clothing advice is the standard one: a moisture-wicking base layer, an insulated middle layer and a breathable outer shell; waterproof boots with moisture-wicking socks under wool socks; a hat, scarf and a spare pair of gloves.

A few things that help on top of that:

- **Dress cooler for the walk out.** Open your jacket and carry the heavy layer. Arriving sweaty is how you get cold an hour later.
- **Get off the ice surface.** Sit on a bucket with a foam pad, and stand on a piece of cardboard or a foam mat. Your feet lose heat to the ice through your boots.
- **Block the wind.** A cheap pop-up shelter or even a sled turned on its side makes a big difference. If you use any heater in an enclosed shelter, use one rated for indoor use, keep it ventilated and carry a carbon monoxide detector.
- **Bring hot food and water.** A thermos of soup is better gear than a new jig.
- **Keep your phone in an inside pocket.** Phone batteries drain fast in the cold, and the phone is part of your safety plan.

## Ice etiquette

The ice gets crowded on good bites, and a few unwritten rules keep it friendly.

- **Give people room.** Do not drill right next to someone else's holes or shelter. If you can hear their conversation, you are probably too close.
- **Drill before you settle, and not next to shelters.** An auger is loud under the ice, and people inside fish houses notice.
- **Do not drive or walk through someone's tip-up spread.** Go around.
- **Pack everything out.** Minnesota's rules say garbage, dead fish and waste may not be left on the ice and must be kept in a container secured to a shelter or sled. Bait containers, line and cans count.
- **Mark or avoid old holes.** Refrozen holes are thin spots. Step around them, and fill or mark large holes you cut if your state asks for it.

## Licenses for ice fishing

Ice fishing needs the same fishing license as open water. Two timing notes matter in winter:

- **Minnesota:** the 2026 license year runs March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027. A resident annual is $25 and a nonresident annual is $51 under the 2026 regulations. Fish houses and dark houses need a **shelter license**, and a portable shelter needs one if you leave it unattended between midnight and one hour before sunrise or go more than 200 feet from it. Residents 16 and older can fish without a license during **Take a Kid Ice Fishing Weekend, January 16 to 18, 2027**, if they fish with a child under 16. Details on our [Minnesota fishing license page](/fishing/minnesota/fishing-license).
- **Michigan:** one all-species license covers ice fishing, and the 2026 regulations run until March 31, 2027. Prices, ages and the February Free Fishing Weekend are on our [Michigan fishing license page](/fishing/michigan/fishing-license).
- **Everywhere else:** start at our [fishing license hub](/fishing-license) and follow the link to your state agency.

## Where to start

Pick a mid-sized inland lake with a public access, a known panfish population and plenty of other anglers. Avoid big, wind-blown water, river mouths and anywhere with current on your first trip. Large bays can shift and crack when the wind changes.

If you want a head start, our guides to the [best ice fishing lakes in Minnesota](/blog/best-ice-fishing-lakes-minnesota) and the [best ice fishing lakes in Michigan](/blog/best-ice-fishing-lakes-michigan) list lakes by species, access and how beginner-friendly they are. On the big destination lakes, renting a fish house or booking a half-day with a guide is the fastest way to learn: someone else has checked the ice, drilled the holes and found the fish, and you get to watch how it is done.

## Keeping a record on the ice

Ice anglers are a skeptical crowd when it comes to phones. One r/IceFishing regular put it simply: "fishing is about escaping my screen." Fair enough. But a quick record of what bit, how deep and when is how you find that spot again next winter, and it is how you learn your lake faster than one season at a time. Our post on [what to record in a fishing log](/blog/fishing-log-what-to-record) covers the useful fields.

The catch on the ice is signal. Many lakes have little or no service. If you do not want a phone out at all, a pencil and a waterproof notebook work too; pens freeze.

Your first season goal is simple: learn to read safe ice, catch a few panfish, and come home warm enough to want to go back.
