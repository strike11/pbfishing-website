Search "how thick does ice need to be" and you get a dozen charts that almost agree. That is because they mostly trace back to a few agency guidelines, each a little different, and each written with the same warning on top: thickness is a minimum, not a guarantee. Below are the numbers straight from the agencies we checked in November 2026, what they mean, and what actually keeps people alive on the ice.

<p class="callout warn"><strong>Check first:</strong> No agency measures or certifies the ice on your lake. The Wisconsin DNR says it does not monitor local ice and points anglers to bait shops, clubs and resorts. Ask locally, then check it yourself.</p>

## Ice thickness chart: what the agencies say

These figures are for **new, clear ice** only. Guidance differs slightly by agency, so the table shows each source side by side. "n/a" means that agency does not publish a figure for that activity.

| Activity | Minnesota DNR (2023 poster) | Army Corps, St. Paul District (Dec. 2023) | Connecticut DEEP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stay off | Under 4 in | n/a | Under 4 in |
| Walking, ice fishing on foot | 4 in | 4 in (a person) | 4 in |
| Snowmobile | 5 to 7 in | 5 in | 5 to 7 in (snowmobile or ATV) |
| Side-by-side ATV | 7 to 8 in | n/a | see above |
| Car | 9 to 12 in | 10 to 12 in (small car) | Illegal to drive on the ice in CT |
| Truck | 13 to 17 in | 12 to 18 in (light truck) | Illegal |
| Heavy-duty truck with a wheelhouse shelter | 20 in or more | n/a | Illegal |

Two agencies in the core ice-fishing states take a harder line:

- **Michigan DNR:** there is no reliable inch-thickness that makes ice safe, and your safety is your responsibility. It publishes no chart.
- **Wisconsin DNR:** there is no such thing as 100 percent safe ice, and ice strength cannot be judged by thickness alone. It also publishes no chart.

None of the charts we opened has a separate "medium truck" row; the Minnesota poster jumps from "truck" at 13 to 17 inches to "heavy-duty truck with wheelhouse shelter" at 20 inches or more. If you see a chart online with different categories, check which agency and year it came from.

## White ice, snow ice and slush count half

Clear ice, often with a bluish tint, is the strongest. The Michigan DNR calls clear blue ice the strongest and milky ice, formed from melted and refrozen snow, weak. Connecticut DEEP says white or snow ice is only about half as strong as new clear ice, and the Minnesota DNR's rule of thumb is to **double the thickness guidelines for white ice**.

Two more points from the Michigan DNR are worth knowing:

- **Slush on top** means the ice is not freezing from the bottom, and slush ice is only half as strong as clear ice. Stay off.
- **Snow-covered ice should be considered unsafe.** Snow insulates the ice and slows freezing, so the ice under it is often thin, and a fresh snowfall can even melt existing ice.

In practice, early-season ice is often a few inches of clear ice with a layer of white ice on top. Only count the clear layer at full value. If you drill through 6 inches and 3 of it is white, think of it as closer to 4 and a half inches of clear ice.

## Why ice is never "safe"

Ice thickness changes across a single lake, sometimes within a few steps. The Minnesota DNR's poster puts it as "check as you go." The things that thin ice are mostly invisible from the surface:

- **Current.** Inlets, outlets, narrows, river mouths and channels between lakes stay thin. The Wisconsin DNR singles out inlets, outlets and narrows; the Michigan DNR notes that stronger current means the ice is more likely to give way to open water.
- **Springs.** Groundwater seeping up through the lake bottom keeps ice thin over the spot. Local anglers usually know where they are; ask at the bait shop.
- **Pressure ridges.** Where ice sheets push together and buckle, the ice is cracked and often has open water. The Minnesota DNR says to avoid them.
- **Structure and objects.** Logs, brush, docks and anything frozen into the ice weaken it. Dock bubblers and de-icers leave unsafe ice around them, per the Michigan DNR.
- **Shoreline gaps.** If there is ice on the lake but open water along shore, the Michigan DNR says to be extra cautious.
- **Weather swings.** A sudden cold front can crack ice within half a day, and a warm spell can weaken it over several days as it thaws and refreezes. Ice also weakens with age.

This is why a number from a chart, or from a bait shop's report on a different bay, is only a starting point.

## How to check ice with a spud bar

The Michigan DNR lists three tools for testing ice: a **spud**, a **needle bar** or an **auger**. Connecticut DEEP adds a fourth step: actually measure it.

1. **From shore, chop or drill a test hole** a few feet out and measure the clear ice with a tape or an ice-depth gauge. Connecticut DEEP warns not to judge thickness by how easily a chisel or drill breaks the surface.
2. **Walk with the spud bar.** Every few steps, strike the ice hard ahead of you. If the bar goes through or the ice sounds hollow and soft, turn around and go back the way you came.
3. **Re-measure as you go.** Drill and measure again at intervals, especially when you change depth, cross a point or approach anything listed in the section above.
4. **Look at the ice in the hole.** Clear, hard ice is good. White, layered or slushy ice counts at half strength.

Most ice anglers carry a spud bar through first ice and late ice, even if they drill fishing holes with an auger.

## What to carry on the ice

The agencies agree closely here.

| Item | Why | Recommended by |
|---|---|---|
| Ice picks or ice claws, worn around the neck | The only practical way to pull yourself out of a hole | MN, MI, WI, CT |
| Life jacket or float suit | Keeps your head up if you go through; the Minnesota DNR advises buoyant gear except inside an enclosed vehicle | MN, MI, WI, CT |
| Rope (a throw rope of 50 ft or so) | Reaching a partner without going out yourself | WI, CT |
| Phone in a waterproof case, or another two-way device that gets a signal | Calling for help | MI, WI, CT |
| Ice cleats or spiked boots | Footing, especially when getting back up | CT, MI (gear list) |
| Whistle | Calling attention when your hands are cold | CT |
| Spud bar or auger | Testing the ice | MI, CT |

Also: tell someone where you are going and when you will be back, do not go alone, and avoid unfamiliar ice at night or in poor visibility, all from the Wisconsin DNR. If you drive on ice where it is legal, the Minnesota DNR says to drive with a window down and the doors unlocked so you can get out.

## What to do if you go through

The Michigan, Wisconsin and Connecticut agencies give nearly the same steps:

1. **Stay calm.** The first minute is cold shock and gasping. Control your breathing.
2. **Keep your winter clothing on.** Heavy clothes trap air, which adds warmth and flotation; they will not drag you down.
3. **Turn back toward the way you came.** That ice held you, so it is likely the strongest.
4. **Get your arms onto the ice and use your picks.** Dig the points in and kick your legs hard to slide forward onto the surface.
5. **Do not stand up.** Lie flat and roll away from the hole to spread your weight.
6. **Get warm and dry immediately.** Get to shelter, change into dry clothes and drink something warm, without alcohol or caffeine.
7. **Call 911** if you feel disoriented, shiver uncontrollably or have any signs of hypothermia, which the Michigan DNR calls a life-threatening condition.

If someone else goes through, call 911 and reach or throw from solid ice with a rope, a pole or a branch. Do not run to the hole.

## The bottom line

Use the chart as a floor. Stay off under 4 inches of clear ice, double it for white ice, check as you go, and carry picks and a flotation layer every time.
