Ask ten anglers for the best time to fish and at least one will say "whenever you can get out." They're not wrong. A trip at a bad hour beats no trip. But if you get to choose, some factors move the odds a lot and some barely move them. An r/Fishing regular summed up forecasts and moon charts nicely: "As a reference point? Sure. As Gospel? No."

This guide ranks what matters, explains why, and gives you the best times for bass, crappie, catfish, trout and walleye. Every claim about fish biology below comes from state agencies, federal habitat reports or peer-reviewed catch studies. The sources are listed at the bottom.

## What matters most, ranked

| Rank | Factor | Why it matters | What to do with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Water temperature and season | Sets how fast fish digest, how far they roam, and when they spawn | Know the water temp and what your species does at that number |
| 2 | Light | Predators feed best at low light. Dawn, dusk, clouds and night all count | Plan around the first and last hour of daylight, or fish at night in summer |
| 3 | A change in the weather | Fronts change light, wind and shallow water temps together | Fish ahead of a front. Slow down for a day or two after one |
| 4 | Wind | Breaks up light, pushes baitfish, mixes water | Start on the bank the wind blows into, if you can fish it safely |
| 5 | Moon and solunar periods | Effects show up in some big datasets, but they're small | Use it as a tiebreaker, never as the reason to stay home |

Two things that aren't on the list matter more than everything on it: where you fish and how you fish. In a long-running Wisconsin creel study covering 2003 to 2015, guide use and live bait were the strongest predictors of walleye and muskie catch. Barometric pressure didn't make a significant difference for either species ([Shaw et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257882)). In a German pike study, heavy fishing pressure over the previous two days cut catch rates more than any weather factor did ([Kuparinen et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fishres.2010.03.011)). So a fresh, lightly fished spot at a so-so hour often beats a hammered community hole at the perfect one.

## Why water temperature comes first

Fish are cold-blooded. Their body runs at the temperature of the water, so water temperature sets their appetite. Federal habitat reports put optimal growth for adult largemouth bass at 24–30°C (75–86°F), with very little growth below 59°F. Walleye grow best at 68–75°F. Rainbow trout do best between 54 and 64°F, and they stop growing at about 73°F ([USFWS habitat suitability reports](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/fwsobs82_10_16)).

That's why "season" really means "temperature." A Texas bass in February and a Minnesota bass in late May can be doing the same thing, because the water is at the same number. Texas Parks and Wildlife says largemouth spawning starts at about 60°F, and that can be anywhere from February to May depending on where you are in the state. Our [water temperature guide](/blog/water-temperature-and-fish-activity) has a full chart by species. Your state's calendar is on its seasons page, for example [Texas](/fishing/texas/fish-species-seasons), [Florida](/fishing/florida/fish-species-seasons), [Tennessee](/fishing/tennessee/fish-species-seasons), [Michigan](/fishing/michigan/fish-species-seasons) and [Minnesota](/fishing/minnesota/fish-species-seasons).

<p class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> The trend matters as much as the number. Water at 58°F and climbing after three warm days fishes very differently from water at 58°F that dropped from 64°F overnight.</p>

## The golden hour: dawn and dusk

The golden hour is the hour or so around sunrise and sunset. It's the closest thing fishing has to a rule that holds up.

- **Largemouth bass.** The federal habitat report on largemouth describes their feeding as bimodal, with peaks in the early morning and late evening.
- **Walleye.** Walleye feed most actively under low light. By day they hold under cover in water shallower than about 50 feet, then move inshore at night to feed. In the Escanaba Lake study, walleye trips were more successful at dawn and dusk than at midday, and the catch rate was higher at dusk.
- **Pike.** The German study found pike catch rates went up at dusk.

Low light helps a predator get close to prey without being seen. That's why an overcast day or a choppy surface can stretch the golden hour into a golden afternoon. A bright, calm, cloudless day after a front shrinks it to a few minutes.

## Is it better to fish at night or in the morning?

It depends on what you're after and what month it is.

**Night wins for:**
- **Channel catfish.** The Missouri Department of Conservation says adults rest in deep water or around drift piles and logs during the day. At night they move onto riffles and into the shallows to feed.
- **Walleye**, especially in clear lakes, where daylight pushes them deep or under cover.
- **Summer bass** on lakes with heavy daytime boat traffic and surface temps in the upper 80s.
- **Summer crappie** under lights from a dock or anchored boat.

**Morning wins for:**
- **Trout in summer.** When Montana rivers hit 73°F for three days in a row, the state bans fishing from 2 p.m. to midnight on those stretches. The state says water at 77°F or warmer can kill trout ([Montana FWP, July 2026](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/MTFWP/bulletins/4217b87)). Cool mornings are when trout feed and when they recover best after release.
- **Anyone fishing topwater** for bass on calm water.

**Afternoon wins** in late fall, winter and early spring. Shallow water warms a few degrees by mid-afternoon on a sunny day, and in cold water a few degrees matters.

If you fish after dark, wear a life jacket, run proper boat lights, and check that your spot is open at night. Many parks close at dusk.

## Best time to fish by species

| Species | Best season | Best time of day | Water temp cue (°F) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Largemouth bass | Spring pre-spawn and spawn, then fall | Dawn and dusk. Night in summer | Spawning starts near 60 | Feeding peaks early and late |
| Crappie | Spring spawn | Dusk and after dark, morning in spring | White crappie nest at 65–70, some as low as 56 | Hold near brush, docks and standing timber |
| Channel catfish | Late spring through summer | Night | Spawning begins near 75 | Rest deep by day, feed shallow at night |
| Rainbow trout | Spring and fall. Winter in stocked southern waters | Morning, overcast days | Comfortable 54–64. Stressed above about 70 | Afternoons in hot weeks can be harmful to fish |
| Walleye | Spring after the spawn, then fall | Dusk, night, dawn | Spawning peaks at 42–50 | Light-shy. Choppy or stained water helps |

### Largemouth bass

Spring is when big females move shallow, so many anglers set personal bests then. Fall is the other strong season, as bass chase baitfish into creek arms while the water cools. In summer, fish the first two hours of light, then move deeper or into shade, or come back at night. More in our [largemouth bass guide](/species/largemouth-bass).

### Crappie

Texas Parks and Wildlife says white crappie nest when the water reaches 65–70°F, and spawning has been seen as low as 56°F. That window gets anglers on the bank faster than anything else all year. After the spawn, crappie spread out to brush piles and timber, and the bite shifts toward evening and night. See the [crappie guide](/species/crappie).

### Channel catfish

Summer nights are prime time. Fish creek channels, the downstream ends of riffles, and flats next to deep water. Missouri puts the start of spawning at 75°F, around late May there. The [channel catfish guide](/species/channel-catfish) covers baits.

### Rainbow trout

In much of the South, rainbows are stocked in winter because the water is cold enough then and too warm in summer. Up north and in tailwaters, spring and fall are best. In summer, go early and skip the hot afternoons. More in the [rainbow trout guide](/species/rainbow-trout).

### Walleye

Walleye are the clearest example of light mattering. Clear lakes fish best at dusk and after dark. Stained water and a good chop on a windy day can make midday productive. Minnesota DNR says walleye spawning peaks at 42–50°F. More in the [walleye guide](/species/walleye).

## Does weather affect fishing?

Yes. But in the data, the effects are smaller and less consistent than fishing TV suggests.

- **Fronts.** A cold front changes light, wind direction and shallow water temperature all at once. The day before often fishes well. The first day or two after is often slow. Our guide to [fishing after rain, wind and cold fronts](/blog/fishing-after-rain-wind-cold-front) covers what to do in each case.
- **Barometric pressure.** Anglers swear by it, but the Wisconsin study found it wasn't significant for walleye or muskie. Fish moving a few feet up or down feel a bigger pressure change than any storm brings. The full argument is in our [barometric pressure guide](/blog/barometric-pressure-and-fishing).
- **Wind.** Pike catch rates went up with wind speed in Germany. In Wisconsin, muskie catch rates dropped from about 0.15 fish per hour in calm air to 0.09 in strong wind. Wind helps or hurts depending on the species and the water.
- **Air temperature.** In the Escanaba data, walleye catch per hour was a little higher on cold days than hot ones.

Weather mostly decides where fish are and how hard they'll chase. It rarely decides whether they eat at all.

## How to pick the best time to fish today

1. **Check the water temperature.** If you don't have a reading, use the last three days of air temperature as a rough guide. Then look up what your target species does at that number.
2. **Find the low-light windows.** Write down today's sunrise and sunset. Clouds or rain stretch those windows. Bluebird skies shrink them.
3. **Look at the three-day weather trend.** Stable or warming is good. The day before a front is often good. The day right after a hard front is usually the slowest.
4. **Check the wind.** Moderate wind helps most species. Pick a spot where the wind blows into the bank and you can still fish safely.
5. **Use the moon last.** If two days look equal, take the one closer to a new or full moon. The evidence for this is weak. Our [solunar tables explainer](/blog/do-solunar-tables-work) has the details.

## Build your own "best time" chart

General rules get you started. Your own log tells you the truth about your lake. Every time you catch a fish, write down the time, the water temperature, the sky, the wind, and whether a front just passed. Do it for skunked trips too, even if it's one line in a notes app. Those blank days are the data most anglers never keep.
