Rain, wind and fronts are the reasons most trips go well or badly. The good news is that fish respond to them in fairly predictable ways. You won't catch fish every time, but you'll know where to start and what to change when the weather turns.

Below is a quick-reference table, then each condition in detail. The biology comes from federal habitat reports, state agencies and peer-reviewed studies, listed at the bottom. The tactics are the ones experienced anglers lean on.

## Quick guide: conditions, where fish go, what to throw

| Condition | What changes underwater | Where to look | What to throw |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light, warm rain | Less light, broken surface, a little cooling | Shallow cover, points, flats | Topwater, spinnerbaits, swim jigs |
| After heavy rain | Runoff, current, stain, food washing in | Creek mouths, inflows, mudlines, eddies | Bladed jigs, spinnerbaits, crankbaits, cut bait for catfish |
| Muddy water | Visibility drops to inches | Shallow and tight to hard cover | Colorado-blade spinnerbaits, rattling cranks, black and blue jigs, bright chartreuse |
| Windy day | Plankton and baitfish pile up downwind. Light is broken | Wind-blown points and banks | Crankbaits, spinnerbaits, jerkbaits, walleye rigs on rocks |
| Before a cold front | Clouds, warm south wind, falling pressure | Shallow, roaming fish | Fast-moving baits. Cover water |
| Day 1 to 2 after a front | Bright skies, cold northwest wind, cooler shallows | Thick cover, deep edges next to shallow spots | Texas rigs, jigs, drop shots, smaller baits fished slowly |
| Bluebird day | High sun, clear water, calm | Shade, docks, deeper weed edges | Finesse baits, skipping jigs under docks, early and late topwater |
| Heat wave | Warm surface, less oxygen, stressed fish | Dawn and dusk, night, current, shade, the thermocline | Night baits, deep cranks, live bait. Handle fish fast |

## Fishing in the rain

A light rain is one of the best conditions you can get. Clouds and drops on the surface cut the light, so bass and walleye hunt in shallow water longer than on a sunny day. Rain also adds a little oxygen and washes insects and worms into the water.

Temperature matters. In early spring, a cold rain can chill shallow water a few degrees and slow fish down. In summer, a warm rain cools the surface slightly, which often perks fish up.

Thunderstorms are the exception. The National Weather Service counted 418 U.S. lightning deaths from 2006 through 2019. Forty happened while people were fishing, more than any other leisure activity and four times as many as golf. On a boat, you're often the tallest thing around. When you hear thunder, get off the water.

## Fishing after rain

After a hard rain, food and stained water pour into the lake from creeks and ditches. Fish move up to meet it.

- **Inflows and creek mouths.** Fish the current seams, eddies and the spot where moving water slows down.
- **Mudlines.** Where muddy runoff meets clearer lake water, predators sit on the clear side and ambush baitfish at the edge. Run a crankbait or spinnerbait right along the line.
- **Rising rivers.** Catfish feed hard on rising water. Fish slack water behind wood and in eddies. Stay off fast-rising rivers in a small boat, and never wade into fast muddy water.

If the whole lake turns to chocolate milk, give it a day or two. The bite usually picks up as the water starts to clear.

## How to fish muddy water

Murky water isn't as bad as it looks. In a lab study, largemouth bass caught just as many fathead minnows at moderate turbidity (18 and 37 NTU) as in clear water (1 NTU). Only in very muddy water (70 NTU) did catches drop. In two Lake Ontario wetlands, bass caught prey just as well in murky water as clear ([Reid, Fox & Whillans, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1139/f99-056)).

What does change is how far a fish will move to eat. So:

- **Fish shallow and tight to cover.** Fish hold right against wood, rock and docks. Hit the cover.
- **Use vibration and noise.** A Colorado-blade spinnerbait, a bladed jig or a rattling square-bill puts out vibration that fish feel through their lateral line.
- **Use high-contrast colors.** Black and blue, or bright chartreuse, show up better than natural shad colors.
- **Slow down.** Give fish time to find the bait.

Walleye are a special case. The federal habitat report on walleye says they feed best when you can see about 3 to 6.5 feet down. Feeding drops off a lot when visibility is under about 3 feet or over 16 feet. So a little stain is great for walleye. Mud isn't.

Catfish don't mind muddy water at all. Channel catfish do well in murky rivers, and they find food by smell and taste. Muddy water is a great time to soak cut bait.

## Wind is your friend (usually)

"Wind is your friend" is old advice, and it mostly holds up. Wind does three useful things:

1. **It breaks up light.** A chop on the surface works like cloud cover for fish below.
2. **It moves food.** Wind pushes plankton toward the downwind shore, baitfish follow it, and predators follow the baitfish. Hal Schramm, a retired fisheries researcher writing for In-Fisherman, notes the main effect of wind is on the shoreline it blows into, through current and plankton.
3. **It mixes and cools the surface** in summer, which helps oxygen.

**Which bank to fish:** start on the bank the wind is blowing into, especially points, rocky shorelines and the ends of islands. That's where baitfish pile up. Run crankbaits, spinnerbaits and jerkbaits parallel to the bank.

**When to fish the calm side instead:** after a cold front, when the wind is cold and out of the northwest, the protected bank often has warmer, calmer shallow water. Fish there, especially in early spring.

The research is mixed, which is a good reminder that wind isn't magic. In a German lake, pike catch rates went up with wind speed ([Kuparinen et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fishres.2010.03.011)). On Escanaba Lake in Wisconsin, muskie catch rates dropped from about 0.15 fish per hour in calm air to 0.09 in winds of 20 mph and up ([Shaw et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257882)). Part of that is simply that big wind makes boat control and lure control hard.

<p class="callout warn"><strong>Check first:</strong> A wind-blown bank is a lee shore. In a kayak or small boat, whitecaps and a rocky windward bank are a bad mix. Wear a life jacket, and if in doubt, fish the wind from the shore.</p>

## Before a cold front

The day or two before a front is often the best fishing of the week. Pressure falls, clouds build, a warm south or southwest wind picks up, and fish roam. This is the time for reaction baits and covering water. Fish fast and shallow, and be ready to leave when the storm line shows up. For the science on pressure, see our [barometric pressure guide](/blog/barometric-pressure-and-fishing).

## After a cold front

After the front passes, the sky clears, the wind swings to the northwest, the air turns cold and pressure climbs. Fishing usually gets tough for one to three days. A 2023 University of Nebraska–Lincoln undergraduate thesis fished for largemouth bass before and just after forecast fronts, and found bass fed noticeably less right after the front passed. The fish that still bit were mostly smaller ones. It's a small student study, but it matches what tournament anglers report.

Why it happens is still debated. Schramm points out that air temperature drops don't reach far into the water, and that pressure changes are small. Light, wind and shallow-water cooling are more likely causes. Either way, here's what tends to work:

- **Fish tighter to cover.** Flip a Texas rig or jig into the thickest grass, wood or docks you can find.
- **Try the first drop-off.** Fish often slide only a little deeper. Check the nearest deeper edge next to where they were yesterday.
- **Downsize and slow down.** Shaky heads, drop shots, small swimbaits and smaller jigs.
- **Fish the afternoon.** By 2 or 3 p.m., shallow water has warmed the most. In spring, that can be the only bite of the day.
- **Don't abandon a working pattern too fast.** Several pros in Schramm's piece said they give yesterday's pattern a fair try before changing everything.

In fall, the first cold front after summer can actually help. It cools the surface and gets fish feeding. In spring, fronts often push bass off their beds for a day or two.

## Bluebird days

A bluebird day means a cloudless sky, bright sun and often little wind, usually right after a front. Light-shy fish go deeper or tuck into cover. The federal walleye report says walleye actively look for dim light on bright days in clear water. They hold deep, in stained water, or under rocks, logs and thick weeds.

Fish the first and last hours of light. At midday, skip jigs under docks, fish the shady side of anything, work deep weed edges with a drop shot, or head to stained water. If you can find wind, fish it.

## Heat waves

In a heat wave, the problem is oxygen as much as temperature. Warm water holds less oxygen than cool water. The federal habitat report on largemouth bass says their growth drops when dissolved oxygen falls below 8 mg/L, distress shows at 5 mg/L, and levels below 1 mg/L kill them.

Lakes also stack up in layers in summer. The Missouri Department of Conservation explains that a pond's cold bottom layer can run out of oxygen completely by mid-July, so almost all fish live in the warm top layer. In a heat wave, fish get squeezed between hot water up top and dead water below. Look for them near the thermocline, in shade, near current, at inflows and near springs.

- **Fish dawn, dusk and night.** Summer bass, walleye and catfish all feed well after dark.
- **Shorten fights and handling time.** Keep the fish in the water when you unhook it, and release it fast. Our [catch-and-release guide](/blog/how-to-release-a-fish) covers technique.
- **Give trout a break.** When Montana rivers hit 73°F for three days in a row, the state bans fishing there from 2 p.m. to midnight. It says water at 77°F or warmer can kill trout ([Montana FWP, July 2026](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/MTFWP/bulletins/4217b87)). Wherever you fish, quit trout fishing by midday when water temperatures reach the 70s.

## Keep a weather log

The patterns above are where to start, not rules. Your lake has its own habits. One reservoir turns on in a south wind, another goes dead. The only way to learn yours is to write it down: rain in the last 24 hours, water clarity, wind direction and speed, and how many days since the last front. Include the days you got skunked.
