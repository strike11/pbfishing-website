"The total lack of activity and action is disappointing." That's a beginner on r/FishingForBeginners after a few empty trips, and almost everyone has been there. The internet's usual answer is "the best time to fish is when you can," which is true and useless. Here are twelve concrete reasons you're not catching fish, roughly in order of how often they're the culprit, with what to change for each.

## 1. You're fishing empty water

Pro angler and In-Fisherman co-founder Al Lindner told Minnesota Public Radio in 1988 that "darn near 90%" of a lake has no fish at a given time. That's the idea behind the [90/10 rule](/blog/90-10-rule-fishing), and it's the single biggest reason anglers go fishless. Open, flat, featureless water is mostly empty.

**Fix:** Cast at *something*. Points, weed edges, docks, laydowns, rock piles, creek mouths, bridge pilings, the edge where shallow flats drop into deeper water. If you can't see anything, look at the bank: a steep bank usually means deeper water close in; a gravel point usually keeps going underwater. Our guide to [finding fishing spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots) shows how to scout this from your phone before you leave home.

## 2. Your bait is at the wrong depth

Bank anglers often fish a bobber set at two feet all year. That works for bluegill on spawning beds in May. In July, when surface water hits the mid-80s °F, and in winter when it's in the 40s, many fish sit in deeper, more stable water that a 2-foot bobber never reaches.

**Fix:** Change depth before you change anything else. Move the bobber up a foot at a time, or switch to a slip bobber so you can fish 6 to 12 feet from the bank. With lures, count them down: cast, count to 3, 6 and 10 before retrieving, and note which count gets bit. Our breakdown of [water temperature and fish activity](/blog/water-temperature-and-fish-activity) shows where fish tend to sit at each temperature.

## 3. You're fishing the wrong hours

Midday on a bright summer day is the toughest window for shallow fish. Light pushes them deeper or tight into shade, and boat traffic peaks.

**Fix:** In summer, fish the first two hours after sunrise and the last two before dark. In spring and fall, midday is often fine because the water is warming. In winter, the warmest afternoon hours can be the best. The [best time to fish](/blog/best-time-to-fish) guide goes deeper on this, and it's honest about which factors are proven and which aren't.

## 4. Your line is too heavy or too visible

20 lb hi-vis line on a tiny jig in gin-clear water looks like a rope. Fish that have been caught before notice.

**Fix:** For finesse baits and panfish in clear water, use 4 to 8 lb line, and consider fluorocarbon or a fluorocarbon leader of 3 to 6 feet. Save heavy line and braid for thick grass, wood and frogs, where you need the strength and visibility matters less.

## 5. Your hook or bait is too big

A size 1/0 hook with a whole nightcrawler is a catfish rig, not a bluegill rig. Panfish will pick the worm off all day without getting hooked, and you'll think nothing is biting.

**Fix:** For panfish, go down to a size 8 or 10 hook and a piece of worm the size of your fingernail. For bass that are short-striking, switch from a 7-inch worm to a 4-inch one, or from a 1/2 oz spinnerbait to 3/8 oz. Downsizing is the most reliable fix on pressured water.

## 6. You're retrieving too fast (or too slow)

Fish metabolism follows water temperature. Below about 50 °F, bass and most panfish won't chase a fast-moving lure. In summer they'll hit a fast crankbait they would ignore in February.

**Fix:** In cold water, slow down until it feels too slow, then go slower. Pause jerkbaits for 5 to 10 seconds. In warm water, speed up and cover water. If you're not sure, vary the speed on every cast and let the fish tell you.

## 7. You're getting bites and missing them

Lots of anglers who say they "never get bites" are actually getting them and not recognizing them: a bobber that tilts but doesn't sink, a slight tick on a soft plastic, line that jumps or swims sideways.

**Fix:** Watch your line, not just your rod tip. Keep slack out of the line so you can feel. Check your hook point on your thumbnail: if it doesn't catch and grab, retie or sharpen. On a bite, reel down until you feel weight and then sweep the rod firmly. Don't wait for a fish to "take it" for half a minute; it'll swallow the hook or drop the bait.

## 8. You're spooking the fish before you cast

Shallow fish feel footsteps on a wooden dock, see shadows and hear tackle boxes slammed on a boat deck. Wading straight into the spot you plan to fish pushes everything off it.

**Fix:** Approach quietly, stand back from the edge, and make your first casts parallel to the bank before you walk up to it. Wear muted colors on clear, shallow water. Fish the near water first, then the far water.

## 9. You're sitting on a dead spot too long

There's loyalty, and there's stubbornness. If a spot has given you nothing for an hour, the fish either aren't there or aren't eating.

**Fix:** Give a spot 15 to 20 minutes on the bank (10 to 15 in a boat), after trying two depths and two baits. Then move. Moving 100 yards along a bank to a different type of cover is often worth more than any lure change. Write down where you've been so you can come back at a different time of day.

## 10. You're fishing the community hole

The pier at the park, the bank next to the parking lot, the spot with a geotagged photo from last weekend. These spots get hammered, and the fish there have seen every worm and spinner in the store. Spot burning on apps and social media has made this worse, a common complaint on r/bassfishing.

**Fix:** Walk farther than other people do. The bank 300 yards from the parking lot gets a fraction of the pressure. Fish at off-peak times, like weekday evenings. And when you find something good, keep it to yourself.

## 11. You're ignoring the wind

Many anglers fish the calm, sheltered bank because it's comfortable. Wind pushes plankton and baitfish onto the windward bank, breaks up the surface so fish are less spooky, and often turns on a feeding window.

**Fix:** Fish the windblown side of points and banks when it's safe to do so, with heavier baits that cast into the wind: spinnerbaits, lipless crankbaits and chatterbaits. Our article on [fishing after rain, wind and cold fronts](/blog/fishing-after-rain-wind-cold-front) covers what changes and why.

## 12. You're not keeping track of anything

This one doesn't catch fish today; it catches them next month. If you can't say what the water temperature, wind and depth were on your last three good days, you're starting from zero every trip.

**Fix:** Log every trip, including the skunks: date, water, time, weather, depth, bait, and where fish were. After five or six trips, patterns jump out, like "all my bass came on the north bank on a south wind." Our guide to [what to record in a fishing log](/blog/fishing-log-what-to-record) has a template you can print. If you'd rather not type it, PB Fishing logs the time, water, weather and pressure with each catch photo automatically, but a notebook works too.

## When it really isn't you

Sometimes the fish just aren't eating. The day after a hard cold front with bluebird skies and a sharp rise in pressure is tough everywhere. Water that has turned over in fall can be dead for a week. Big rain can muddy a small pond for days. Two honest checks:

- **Is anyone else catching?** Ask at the bait shop or look at the parking lot crowd. If everyone is struggling, it's conditions. If one guy is catching, watch what he's doing and how deep.
- **Are you seeing fish at all?** Baitfish flicking, bluegill under docks, bass busting shad. If you see life but can't get bit, it's presentation (fixes 4 to 7). If you see nothing, it's location (fixes 1 and 2).

## A quick diagnosis table

| Symptom | Most likely cause | First thing to change |
|---|---|---|
| No bites at all, no fish seen | Empty water or wrong depth | Move to structure, change depth |
| Bait comes back chewed, no hookups | Hook too big, slow hookset | Smaller hook, watch the line |
| Bites early, then nothing | Light changed or fish moved | Go deeper or to shade |
| Others catching, you aren't | Presentation | Copy their depth and speed, downsize |
| Nobody catching | Conditions (front, turnover, mud) | Slow down, fish deeper, accept a slow day |
| Same spot was great last month | Season moved the fish | Follow them deeper or shallower |

Being skunked is part of fishing. Being skunked the same way five times in a row is a pattern, and patterns can be fixed. If you're new, start with the [beginner checklist](/blog/fishing-for-beginners), pick one fix from this list per trip, and write down what happened.
