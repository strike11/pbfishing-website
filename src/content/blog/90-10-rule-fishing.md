"What's the 90/10 rule in fishing?" shows up in Google's People Also Ask on searches for fishing apps, Fishbrain reviews and bass fishing. It's a good question with a short answer and a longer, more useful one. Here are both, plus the three versions of the 80/20 rule, and a way to find your own 10% that doesn't depend on anyone else's pins.

## The 90/10 rule in one sentence

About 90% of the fish in a lake or river are in about 10% of the water at any given time.

The corollary matters more than the rule: **most of the water you could cast at holds no fish right now.** If you spread your time evenly over a lake, you spend most of it fishing empty water. The anglers who catch fish consistently aren't magic with a lure; they waste less time in the 90%.

## Where it comes from

The idea is old, and nobody owns it. The earliest firm reference we found is Al Lindner, the pro angler and co-founder of In-Fisherman, in a Minnesota Public Radio profile that aired May 14, 1988. Lindner said that even on good lakes "darn near 90%" of the water has no fish at a given time and only 10% is holding fish. The In-Fisherman school of thought leaned hard on the same idea: fish relate to structure, and the structure that holds them changes with the calendar.

You'll see other attributions online, including books by divers who said they saw the same clustering underwater. We couldn't verify those, so we're not repeating them. The honest description is that the 90/10 rule is folk wisdom that experienced anglers converged on, not a statistic from a study.

## The 80/20 rule in bass fishing

The 80/20 rule is borrowed from the Pareto principle (the observation that a small share of causes often produces most of the effects). In bass fishing it gets used for three different ideas, which is why it's confusing:

| Version | What it claims | What it's good for |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 80% of bass are in 20% of the water | Same as 90/10, slightly less extreme. Useful on smaller lakes and ponds. |
| Anglers | 20% of anglers catch 80% of the fish | A reminder that skill and time on the water matter more than gear. |
| Time and lures | Fish proven, confidence baits 80% of the time, experiment 20% | Keeps you catching while still learning. |

The third version is the most practical day to day. If a Texas-rigged worm and a spinnerbait have caught most of your bass, spend most of your casts on them, and give a new lure a fair, limited test rather than a whole day.

## Why fish cluster

Fish aren't spread evenly because the things they need aren't. The 10% is where several of these overlap:

- **Food.** Baitfish, crawfish, insects. Find the shad and you've found the bass.
- **Cover.** Weeds, wood, docks, rock. Ambush points for predators and shelter for prey.
- **Depth change.** Drop-offs, channel edges, points. Fish can move shallow to feed and deep to rest without traveling far.
- **Comfortable water.** The right temperature and enough oxygen. In summer, many lakes stratify, and the deep water below the thermocline can be low in oxygen, so fish compress into a band.
- **Current and wind.** Moving water delivers food. Windblown banks collect plankton and the baitfish that eat it.

A spot with one of these is okay. A spot with three is the 10%. A weed edge on a point that drops into 15 feet, with shad on it and wind blowing in, will hold fish when the rest of the lake looks dead.

## The 10% moves

This is where people misuse the rule. The 10% isn't a fixed location; it changes with season, temperature and weather. A rough guide for largemouth bass:

| Season | Water temp | Where the 10% tends to be |
|---|---|---|
| Winter | Below 50 °F | Deep channel bends, steep bluff banks, deepest points; fish move little |
| Pre-spawn | 50 to 60 °F | Secondary points and the first drop-off outside spawning flats |
| Spawn | 60 to 70 °F | Protected shallow flats and pockets with hard bottom |
| Post-spawn and early summer | 70 to 80 °F | Outside weed edges, docks, first breaks; topwater at dawn |
| Summer | 80 °F and up | Offshore ledges, humps, deep weed lines; shade and current in the shallows |
| Fall | Falling from 75 to 55 °F | Creek arms and backs of pockets following baitfish |

A bank that was the best spot in the lake in May can be empty in August. That's why "my honey hole stopped producing" is usually the season, not the spot. The [best bass lures by season](/blog/best-bass-lures-by-season) guide pairs each of these windows with what to throw.

## How to apply the 90/10 rule on the water

1. **Eliminate water before you fish it.** Before your first cast, look at a map or satellite view and cross off flat, featureless stretches. Circle points, creek mouths, weed lines and drop-offs. Our guide to [finding fishing spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots) shows how to do this with Google Maps and free state agency maps.
2. **Fish fast to find, slow to catch.** Use a moving bait (spinnerbait, crankbait, lipless crankbait) to cover water until you get bit. Then slow down with a worm or jig in that area.
3. **Treat one bite as a clue, not a fluke.** When you catch a fish, note the depth, the cover and what the bank looks like. Then look for the same combination elsewhere on the lake. That's how one fish becomes a pattern.
4. **Set a timer.** If a spot gives you nothing in 15 to 20 minutes after two depths and two baits, move. It's probably the 90% today.
5. **Revisit the good 10% at different times.** A point that's dead at noon can be loaded at dusk.

If you keep getting skunked even on good-looking water, run through the twelve fixes in [why you're not catching fish](/blog/why-am-i-not-catching-fish). Wrong depth is the usual reason good water looks empty.

## How a catch log shows your own 10%

The strongest version of the 90/10 rule is personal. On the lakes you fish, a small number of spots and conditions probably produce most of your fish, and you can prove it with your own records.

Here's a hypothetical example of how that looks after a season of logging a home lake. Every number below is made up to illustrate the method, not taken from real data:

| Spot | Trips fished | Bass caught | Share of catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| North point weed edge | 14 | 23 | 48% |
| Dam riprap | 9 | 11 | 23% |
| Creek mouth | 8 | 6 | 13% |
| Swim beach dropoff | 6 | 4 | 8% |
| Eight other spots, combined | 21 | 4 | 8% |
| **Total** | | **48** | **100%** |

Two spots produce 71% of the fish. The eight "other" spots took 21 visits for 4 bass. That's your 90/10 in your own numbers, and the next step is to ask why: what depth, wind direction, water temperature and time of day did the north point fish have in common?

To do that, each catch needs a few fields: date and time, spot (your own name for it), depth, water temperature if you have it, wind direction, sky, the bait, and the fish. Log the skunked trips too, or your data only shows where you caught fish, not where you wasted time. The full template is in our guide on [what to record in a fishing log](/blog/fishing-log-what-to-record).

## Limits of the rule

- **Small ponds bend it.** In a one-acre farm pond, fish may use most of the water over a day. The rule is most useful on big lakes and reservoirs.
- **Schooling fish break it both ways.** Open-water species like white bass or crappie on a big lake can be in 1% of the water, and that 1% moves daily.
- **Other people's 10% is pressured.** The famous points everyone knows get hammered. Your own 10% is often a less obvious version of the same thing: a smaller point, a deeper weed edge, the back side of a dock row.

The rule isn't magic and the numbers aren't precise. What it teaches is real: stop fishing water because it's in front of you, and start fishing water because something about it should hold fish.
