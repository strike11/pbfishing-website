Every chart you find online gives slightly different numbers, and most don't say where they came from. This one does. It's built from the standard-weight equation fisheries biologists use to judge the condition of bass in state surveys, so you can see the math and check it yourself.

## How this chart works

Biologists describe the length-weight relationship of a species with one equation per species, called a standard-weight (Ws) equation. For largemouth bass the accepted one is Henson (1991), listed in the American Fisheries Society's *Fisheries Techniques* (Neumann, Guy and Willis, 2012) and in the FSA package's table of standard-weight equations:

**log10(W) = −5.528 + 3.273 × log10(L)**, with W in grams and L = total length in millimeters. It applies to fish 150 mm (about 6 inches) and longer.

Here's one row worked out: 20 in = 508 mm → 10^(−5.528 + 3.273 × log10 508) = 10^3.328 ≈ 2,130 g ≈ **4 lb 11 oz**.

One thing to know before you read the numbers: **standard weight is not average weight.** The equation was built from the 75th percentile of many bass populations, so it describes a fish in good shape. Biologists then compare real fish to it with relative weight (Wr): a fish at exactly standard weight is Wr 100. That's why the chart has three columns:

- **Lean (Wr 85):** a thin fish, the kind you see after the spawn or in a lake short on forage.
- **Standard (Wr 100):** a well-fed fish.
- **Heavy (Wr 115):** a fat fish, often a pre-spawn female or a bass living on shad or trout.

### Reading the chart honestly

- **Each inch matters more as fish get bigger.** Going from 14 to 15 inches adds about 6 ounces. Going from 22 to 23 adds a full pound.
- **Above about 24 inches, treat the numbers as rough.** Very few giant bass are in the data sets behind these equations, so the big end of the curve is an extrapolation. A 26-inch Florida-strain female in February can easily beat her row; a long, skinny 26-incher in August can miss it by a couple of pounds.
- **A fish under the "lean" column isn't a bad fish.** It may just have spawned.

That spread is exactly why the 22-inch "no scale" threads on r/bassfishing end with guesses anywhere from 5 to 7 pounds. Everyone's right for some 22-inch fish.

## Adding girth: two formulas compared

Length alone assumes an average body shape. Girth (the distance around the fish at its widest point) tells you how fat this particular fish is. Two formulas show up on state agency pages, both using inches and giving pounds:

- **Bass formula:** length × length × girth ÷ 1,200 (Wisconsin DNR)
- **All-purpose formula:** length × girth × girth ÷ 800 (Wisconsin DNR's trout formula; Texas Parks and Wildlife says it works for all fish and uses a largemouth as its example)

Here's how they compare on a 20-inch largemouth, next to the chart's standard weight of 4 lb 11 oz (4.69 lb):

| Girth | Length² × girth ÷ 1,200 | Length × girth² ÷ 800 |
|---|---|---|
| 12 in | 4.00 lb | 3.60 lb |
| 13 in | 4.33 lb | 4.23 lb |
| 14 in | 4.67 lb | 4.90 lb |
| 15 in | 5.00 lb | 5.63 lb |
| 16 in | 5.33 lb | 6.40 lb |

The two formulas agree when girth is about two-thirds of length (13⅓ inches on a 20-inch fish). Fatter than that and the ÷800 formula climbs fast, because girth is squared. For bass, the ÷1,200 version is the gentler one and lands closer to the standard-weight chart on normal fish; the ÷800 version may read closer on a genuinely football-shaped pre-spawn female. Measure girth snug, not squeezed, just ahead of the dorsal fin.

If you'd rather not do the arithmetic, the [fish weight calculator](/tools/fish-weight-calculator) takes length alone or length and girth, for bass and 20-plus other species.

## Smallmouth bass weight chart

Smallmouth standard weight comes from Kolander and others (1993): log10(W) = −5.329 + 3.200 × log10(L), for fish 150 mm and up. At the same length, a smallmouth weighs almost exactly what a largemouth does. The difference is how long it takes them to get there. Biologists' length categories put a "trophy" smallmouth at 20 inches versus 25 for largemouth.

## When the chart is wrong for your fish

- **Pre-spawn females** (late winter in the South, spring up north) carry eggs and can sit in the heavy column or above it.
- **Post-spawn fish** in late spring are often in the lean column.
- **Lakes with rich forage** (shad, stocked trout, herring) grow fat fish. Small, crowded ponds often grow thin ones.
- **Your measurement.** An open mouth, an unpinched tail or a soft tape over the curve can move you a full row.

When a fish might be a personal best or a lake record, weigh it. A charted weight is an estimate; a scale reading with the display in the photo is a fact.
