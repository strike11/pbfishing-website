Every week somebody on r/bassfishing posts a fish with "22 inches, no scale, what's it weigh?" and the replies spend half the thread arguing about whether it was really 22. Usually the fish was measured over the curve with a soft tape, or with the mouth open, or held out at arm's length for the photo. None of that is cheating. It's just measuring wrong. Here's how to do it right, the way state agencies and tournament directors want it done.

## Total length vs fork length

There are two lengths you'll see in regulations, and they are not the same number.

| Measurement | Where it starts | Where it ends | Pinch the tail? | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Total length (TL)** | Tip of the snout or jaw, mouth closed | Farthest tip of the tail fin | Yes, lobes squeezed together | Nearly all freshwater limits: bass, crappie, walleye, catfish, trout |
| **Fork length (FL)** | Tip of the snout, mouth closed | Center of the fork in the tail | No | Some saltwater species, and the length often used for record-keeping on species with deeply forked tails |

South Carolina DNR defines them in one line each: total length is tip of snout to tip of tail, excluding filaments, with the tail pinched; fork length is tip of snout to fork of tail. Missouri adds that paddlefish are measured from the eye to the fork and sturgeon from the snout to the fork. The lesson: read the rule for your species and state before you decide a fish is legal. Your state's regulation guide says which length applies, and the [Texas](/fishing/texas/fishing-license) and [Florida](/fishing/florida/fishing-license) license pages link straight to them.

## How to measure total length, step by step

Texas Parks and Wildlife's measurement tips are four sentences long, and they cover it:

1. **Lay the fish on its side with the jaw closed.** An open bass mouth pushes the lower jaw forward and adds length that doesn't count.
2. **Pinch the tail.** For soft-tailed fish like bass and crappie, squeeze the tail fin together to get the maximum overall length. For hard-tailed fish, TPWD says to turn the tail to reach maximum length.
3. **Measure in a straight line** from the tip of the snout to the extreme tip of the tail fin.
4. **Read the last line the tail touches.** Texas 4-H tournament rules put it plainly: the last black line the pinched tail touches is the measurement. Don't round up.

Missouri says the same thing with different words: fish laid flat on the ruler, mouth closed, tail lobes pressed together.

### Straight line, not over the curve

This is the mistake behind most inflated lengths. A soft sewing tape laid along the side of a fish follows the body's curve, and the curve is longer than the straight line between nose and tail. On a deep-bodied largemouth it can add a noticeable amount. The legal length is the straight one, so measure on a flat surface, or hold a rigid ruler alongside the fish, not over it.

## Bump boards, tapes and stickers

- **Bump board (measuring board).** A rigid trough with a stop at one end. You push the fish's closed mouth against the "bump" and pinch the tail on the scale. It's the fastest way to get the same number every time, and it's what most kayak and online tournaments require. The Texas 4-H virtual tournaments, for example, require a Check-It-Stik board and a photo of the whole fish on it.
- **Boat-deck sticker or ruler.** Fine for legal-size checks, as long as the fish lies flat and the zero is at a real stop.
- **Soft tape.** Good for girth (see below). Bad for length unless the fish is lying flat and you pull the tape straight above it.

Boards wear and warp. Check yours against a steel tape once a season. And know that altered boards are a real problem: on r/kayakfishing, anglers have described boards with cut sections and modified markings, which is why many tournaments list the exact board models they accept. If your number matters, use a stock board and leave it alone.

## Measuring for legal size

If your state has a 14-inch minimum on bass, a fish is legal when its total length, measured the way above, reaches 14 inches. A few habits keep you out of trouble:

- **Measure before you decide to keep it,** on a flat board, mouth closed, tail pinched.
- **If it's within a hair of the line, release it.** A wildlife officer's board and yours may not agree.
- **Slot limits cut both ways.** A protected slot means fish inside that range must go back, so a fish that measures "just under" the top of the slot is still in the slot.
- **Measure fork-length species to the fork.** Pinching a fork-length fish doesn't help you, because the rule ignores the tail tips.

## How to measure a catfish

Catfish are the hardest fish to measure. They're slick, they roll, and big ones don't fit on most boards. The rules don't change: total length, mouth closed, tail pinched. What changes is how you hold the fish.

- **Lay it on its belly, not its side.** Texas 4-H rules say exactly that for catfish: belly down on the board, then pinch the tail to a point. A cat on its side tends to curl.
- **Wet the board and your hands** so you don't strip slime off the fish.
- **For a fish longer than the board,** Texas 4-H has anglers start the board at the snout and run a tape from the start of the board to the end of the tail, never under the fish, with the whole tape visible in the photo or video.
- **Support big cats under the belly.** Don't hang a heavy flathead or blue by the jaw while you hunt for the tape.

## How to measure girth with a soft tape

Girth is the distance around the fish at its widest point. Wisconsin DNR defines it that way, and it's the second number you need for a length-and-girth weight estimate.

1. Find the fattest part of the body, usually just in front of the dorsal fin on a bass.
2. Slide a soft tape under the fish and around, snug against the skin but not squeezing.
3. Read it in inches to the nearest quarter.
4. If you can only reach one side, South Carolina DNR suggests measuring top to bottom across one side and doubling it. That's rougher, so note that you did it.

Girth swings a lot with a full stomach or eggs, so a pre-spawn female in March and the same fish in July can measure very differently.

## How to measure a fish from a photo

People ask this after the fish is gone, and the honest answer is: you can estimate, but only if the photo contains something of known length lying in the same plane as the fish.

- **Best case:** the fish is lying on a measuring board. Read the board.
- **Workable:** a known object right beside the fish, at the same distance from the camera. A US dollar bill is 6.14 inches long. Scale the fish against it and treat the answer as rough.
- **Not workable:** a fish held out toward the camera. Anything closer to the lens looks bigger, which is the whole trick behind the arms-out "grip and grin." No app or formula can fix missing depth information.

PB Fishing estimates length from the side-profile photo you take when you log a catch, which is handy when you forgot the board. Treat that as an estimate too, and put a board or ruler in the frame when the number matters.

## The side-profile photo that holds up

If you want a length that nobody in the comments can poke holes in, or a shot a tournament director will accept, this is the checklist:

- **Whole fish on the board, flat, on its side** (belly down for catfish).
- **Closed mouth touching the bump.** Don't cover the mouth or tail with your fingers; Texas 4-H rules specifically say not to block those views.
- **Tail pinched** so you can see where the tip lands.
- **Camera straight above the fish,** not at an angle, so the board's numbers stay true.
- **Numbers readable.** Watch for sun glare on the board.
- **Quick.** Board ready, camera open, then the fish comes out of the water. See [how to release a fish](/blog/how-to-release-a-fish) for keeping air time short.

Take the hero shot after the board shot, not instead of it.

## From length to weight

Once you have a good length, you can estimate weight two ways.

**Length only.** Fisheries biologists use species-specific "standard weight" equations built from thousands of fish. They describe a well-fed fish, so a skinny fish will weigh less. The [largemouth bass weight chart](/blog/largemouth-bass-weight-chart) shows the numbers inch by inch, and the [fish weight calculator](/tools/fish-weight-calculator) does it for 20-plus species.

**Length and girth.** Add girth and you account for how fat the fish actually is. Two common formulas, both in inches and pounds:

| Formula | Source | Example: 20 in long, 14 in girth |
|---|---|---|
| Length × length × girth ÷ 1,200 | Wisconsin DNR (bass) | 4.67 lb |
| Length × girth × girth ÷ 800 | Wisconsin DNR (trout); Texas Parks and Wildlife says it works for all fish | 4.90 lb |

Neither replaces a scale. They're good enough to know whether you're looking at a 4 or a 6, which is usually what the argument is about. If you want to put it to the test, try the [guess-the-weight quiz](/quiz/guess-the-weight).
