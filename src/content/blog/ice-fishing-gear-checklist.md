Walk into a sporting goods store in December and the ice aisle will happily sell you $3,000 of gear. You need a fraction of it. This list is in the order we would buy it, safety first, with a **budget** pick and a **nice-to-have** pick for each item. Prices are approximate US retail ranges as of November 2026 and vary by store, size and season; we do not have affiliate links or brand deals, so there are no brand names here.

## 1. A fishing license

Ice fishing needs the same license as open water. In Minnesota, the 2026 license year ends February 28, 2027 (a resident annual is $25 and a nonresident annual $51 under the 2026 regulations); Michigan's 2026 regulations run until March 31, 2027. See [Minnesota](/fishing/minnesota/fishing-license), [Michigan](/fishing/michigan/fishing-license) or [every state](/fishing-license).

- **Budget:** a resident annual license.
- **Nice-to-have:** keep a digital copy on your phone and a printed one in your gear bag.

## 2. Ice picks

Two handles with steel points, joined by a cord, worn around your neck or clipped to your jacket. If you go through, they are how you pull yourself back onto the ice. The Minnesota, Michigan, Wisconsin and Connecticut agencies all recommend them, and the Minnesota DNR says to keep them **immediately accessible**, not in a bucket.

- **Budget:** basic retractable picks, about $10 to $20.
- **Nice-to-have:** picks built into a float suit or jacket, included with the suit.

## 3. A life jacket or float suit

The Minnesota DNR advises buoyant gear on the ice except inside an enclosed vehicle. A plain life jacket under or over your coat works.

- **Budget:** a standard life jacket, about $20 to $60.
- **Nice-to-have:** a flotation ice suit (bibs and jacket), about $300 to $700. It replaces your outer layer and your life jacket in one.

## 4. A throw rope and a whistle

Wisconsin DNR and Connecticut DEEP both list a rope; Connecticut adds a whistle. A rope lets you reach a partner from solid ice without walking up to the hole.

- **Budget:** 50 feet of floating rope in a throw bag, about $15 to $35; a plastic whistle, a few dollars.

## 5. Ice cleats

Wet ice under fresh snow is slick, and falling on a hard surface with a spud bar in hand is how winter trips end in the ER. The Michigan DNR's ice-fishing gear list includes ice cleats, and Connecticut DEEP lists spiked shoes or creepers.

- **Budget:** slip-on rubber cleats with studs, about $20 to $45.

## 6. A spud bar

A heavy steel bar with a chisel end, for testing ice and chopping holes. The Michigan DNR lists a spud, needle bar or auger as the tools to test ice thickness. Hit the ice ahead of you every few steps on the way out; see our [ice thickness guide](/blog/ice-thickness-safety) for how.

- **Budget:** a steel spud bar with a wrist lanyard, about $30 to $70. Tie the lanyard to your wrist so the bar does not go down the hole with your first strike.

## 7. An auger

The auger makes your fishing holes. Hole size matters: **6 inches** is enough for panfish and most walleye, **8 inches** if you set tip-ups for pike or big walleye.

- **Budget:** a 6-inch hand auger, about $60 to $130. Keep the blade cover on and the blades sharp.
- **Nice-to-have:** a power auger (battery, gas or propane), about $300 to $800 or more. Battery models are quieter and lighter. A drill-adapter auger that runs on a high-torque cordless drill costs less if you already own the drill.

## 8. A skimmer and a bucket

Holes freeze over and fill with slush. A skimmer (slotted scoop on a handle) keeps them open; the Michigan DNR lists it with the spud and auger as core tools. A 5-gallon bucket carries your gear and becomes your seat.

- **Budget:** skimmer, about $5 to $15; a plain bucket, about $5.
- **Nice-to-have:** a bucket with a padded swivel lid and side pockets, about $25 to $40.

## 9. An ice rod and line

Ice rods are short, about 24 to 32 inches, because you are sitting over the hole. The Michigan DNR describes them as short, limber rods with reels or simple spring spools.

- **Budget:** one light-action combo spooled with 2 to 4 lb line, about $20 to $60. A light combo covers bluegill, perch and crappie.
- **Nice-to-have:** a second, medium-action rod with 6 to 8 lb line for walleye and jigging spoons, about $30 to $80.

## 10. Jigs, spoons and live bait

Small and few. Winter fish want small, slow baits.

- **Budget:** a dozen 1/32 to 1/16 oz jigs in glow and natural colors, two or three small spoons, split shot, about $15 to $40 in total. Add a container of wax worms or spikes (a few dollars) and, for walleye, a scoop of fathead minnows or shiners from the bait shop.
- **Nice-to-have:** tungsten jigs, which sink faster at a smaller size, and a few soft plastics. Expect to pay more per jig.

The Michigan DNR lists wax worms, spikes, wigglers and minnows as the usual ice baits. Keep minnows from freezing in an insulated bait bucket.

## 11. Tip-ups

A tip-up holds a baited line under a flag that pops up when a fish takes the bait. They let you fish a second line for walleye or pike while you jig. Check your state's line limit before you buy several: Minnesota allows 2 lines through the ice, and you must stay within 200 feet of a tip-up. Michigan's line and marking rules are in its 2026 fishing regulations digest.

- **Budget:** one or two classic wooden or plastic tip-ups, about $10 to $35 each, with braided tip-up line and a few size 8 to 10 treble or octopus hooks.
- **Nice-to-have:** insulated round tip-ups that cover the hole and slow freezing.

## 12. Warm, waterproof clothing

Most of this you may already own. The Michigan DNR's list is the standard: moisture-wicking base layer, insulated middle layer, breathable outer shell, waterproof boots, wool socks over wicking socks, a hat and scarf, and a spare pair of gloves.

- **Budget:** what you have, plus winter boots rated well below zero, about $80 to $200, and spare gloves.
- **Nice-to-have:** the float suit from item 3, heated insoles or gloves, and a foam pad to stand on.

## 13. A flasher, a shelter and a sled

These are the upgrades that change the experience, and the ones to rent or borrow before you buy.

- **Flasher**, about $250 to $700: real-time sonar that shows bottom, your jig and fish rising to it. The single most useful upgrade.
- **Portable shelter**, about $150 to $600: hub-style pop-ups are light; flip-over sleds-with-a-roof are faster to set up. If you use a heater inside, use one rated for indoor use, ventilate it and add a carbon monoxide detector, about $20 to $40. In Minnesota, a portable shelter left unattended between midnight and one hour before sunrise (or when you are more than 200 feet away) needs a shelter license, and shelters on the ice overnight need reflective material on each side.
- **Sled**, about $30 to $100: drags everything out and back. Flip-over shelters often double as one.

## What we would skip in year one

Underwater cameras, big wheelhouses, GPS units and a tackle box full of colors can all wait until you know what you like to fish for. Rent a fish house on a big lake for a day, or go with someone who owns the gear, before you buy any of the nice-to-haves. If you are still choosing a lake, our guides to the [best ice fishing lakes in Minnesota](/blog/best-ice-fishing-lakes-minnesota) and [Michigan](/blog/best-ice-fishing-lakes-michigan) are a good start, and [ice fishing for beginners](/blog/ice-fishing-for-beginners) walks you through the first trip.

One thing you probably already own is a phone, and it belongs in the safety column more than the fishing one. Keep it charged, in a waterproof bag, in an inside pocket where the cold will not drain it.
