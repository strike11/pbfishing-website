Anglers who keep logs say the same two things about them. They love having a record, and after a couple of years, "when you accumulate a lot of info, it's not easy to review," as one ANGLR reviewer put it in the App Store. The fix is to decide in advance what a log is for. It isn't a diary. It's a tool for answering three questions: **where were the fish, under what conditions, and what did they eat?**

## Why bother

Because memory lies. Everyone remembers the 6-pounder on the chartreuse spinnerbait. Nobody remembers that the four trips before it were skunks on the same bait, or that every good day that spring came on a falling barometer with a south wind. A log makes your own [90/10 rule](/blog/90-10-rule-fishing) visible: the few spots, times and conditions that produce most of your fish.

It also fixes the most frustrating problem in fishing. If you keep [not catching fish](/blog/why-am-i-not-catching-fish), a log tells you whether it's the spot, the timing or the technique, instead of guessing.

## The fields that matter

Split them into what you must capture on the water (it changes or you'll forget) and what you can fill in later (it's on a weather site or in your head that evening).

### Capture at the water

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Time of each catch | 6:42 am | Reveals bite windows |
| Spot | "North point, outside weed edge" | Your own names; the heart of the log |
| Depth | 8 ft | The variable anglers most often get wrong |
| Bait and retrieve | 3/8 oz white spinnerbait, slow roll | What worked, and how |
| Species and size | Largemouth, 17.5 in, 2 lb 9 oz | Size trends and personal bests |
| Photo | Side profile, fish near the water | Proof of size, ID and a record you'll enjoy |

### Fill in later

| Field | Example | Where to get it |
|---|---|---|
| Date and start/end time | Oct 12, 6:15–10:30 am | Your phone |
| Water body | Lake Conroe | Memory |
| Water temperature | 71 °F | Your sonar or a thermometer, noted at the start |
| Sky | Overcast, clearing by 9 | Memory or weather history |
| Wind | S 8–12 mph | Weather history |
| Barometric pressure and trend | 29.92 in, falling | Weather history |
| Water clarity and level | Stained, 1 ft low | Your eyes, the lake level site |
| Moon phase | Waxing gibbous | Any calendar |
| Fishing pressure | 6 trucks at the ramp | Your eyes |
| Notes | "Shad busting at 7 on the windblown bank" | The thing you'll want to remember |

Two fields beginners skip that matter the most: **depth** and **the trips with no fish**. Without depth, you can't tell a seasonal move from a dead spot. Without skunks, your log is a highlight reel that overrates every spot you fished on good days.

Fields you can skip unless you're serious about them: air temperature (water temp is what fish feel), humidity, exact solunar times (you can look them up later if you want to test them; see [do solunar tables work](/blog/do-solunar-tables-work)), and line brand.

## How to capture fast without hurting the fish

The fish comes first. Unhook it, take one photo with the fish close to the water, and let it go. Then write. If you want a length, measure on a board or a ruler on the boat deck quickly; our guide to [how to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish) covers doing it right.

At the water, capture only time, spot, depth, bait and fish. Everything else can wait until the evening, when you look up the wind and pressure for that morning. If you're logging with an app, a photo-first flow handles most of this: PB Fishing, for example, saves the date, water and a weather and pressure snapshot with the catch photo, and it stores catches on the phone when you have no signal and uploads them later.

## The monthly review (20 minutes)

This is the step that turns a log into a fishing tool. Once a month, sit down with the log and answer these questions. On paper, use tally marks. In a spreadsheet, use filters or a pivot table.

1. **Catches by spot.** Count fish per spot, and divide by the number of times you fished it. A spot with 10 fish in 2 trips beats one with 12 fish in 9 trips.
2. **Catches by depth.** Did your fish come from a narrow depth band? That band usually shifts with the season.
3. **Catches by bait.** Which baits produced, and which ones did you fish a lot for nothing? Be honest about confidence baits you throw out of habit.
4. **Catches by time of day.** Look for a window. Many anglers find it's narrower than they thought.
5. **Conditions on good vs bad days.** Compare wind direction, sky, pressure trend and water temperature for your three best and three worst trips.
6. **Personal bests.** Note any new PB by species and what produced it.

Then write one or two sentences at the bottom of the month. For example: "October bass came at 6 to 10 ft on the windblown side of points, on moving baits. Post-front bluebird days were dead shallow." Next October, read that first.

Every three months, compare against the same season last year. That's where the real patterns are: lakes repeat seasonal patterns year to year more reliably than they repeat day to day.

## Paper vs spreadsheet vs app: an honest comparison

| | Paper notebook | Spreadsheet | App |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed at the water | Slow with wet hands | Slow on a phone | Fastest, especially photo-first |
| Weather and pressure | You look them up | You look them up | Often filled in automatically |
| Finding patterns | Tedious after a season | Best: filters, pivot tables, charts | Depends on the app; some only show a list |
| Privacy | Total | Total if you don't share the file | Depends on what the app shares; check settings |
| Reliability | Waterproof paper lasts decades | Lasts as long as your account | Apps get discontinued; check data export first |
| Cost | A few dollars | Free | Free to subscription |

**Paper** suits anglers who fish one or two lakes and like the ritual. Use waterproof paper and a pencil. The weakness is review: after 100 entries, finding every trip with a south wind takes an evening.

**Spreadsheet** suits anglers who like numbers. Google Sheets on your phone works at the water in a pinch, but most people jot notes and type it up later. It's the best format for the monthly review.

**App** suits anglers who want the conditions filled in and fast capture. Private, single-purpose logs such as Anglers' Log and Infinite Livewell are praised on Reddit precisely because they share nothing. Bigger apps add maps, forecasts and community features, which is useful but means you should know what's public. Two things to check before you commit years of data to any app: what other users can see, and whether you can get your data out if the app shuts down. Our roundup of [the best fishing log apps](/blog/best-fishing-log-apps) compares them.

Many anglers end up with a hybrid: an app or a notes file for capture at the water, and a spreadsheet or paper summary for the monthly review. Whatever you pick, the best log is the one you still keep in August.

## Keeping your spots private

Your log is valuable precisely because it holds your spots, and spot burning is a real worry. A few rules:

- **Use code names** for spots in anything that might be shared, including screenshots.
- **Strip location from photos** before posting them anywhere public. Most phones let you turn off location for a single share.
- **Know what your app shows.** Read the app's sharing settings, not its marketing. As a concrete example, a PB Fishing leaderboard entry shows the angler's name, state, length and weight. It does not show the lake or coordinates. Anything you post to an app's feed or stories is a separate decision; treat it like any public post and check the photo background.
- **Don't log other people's spots** in shared files without asking.

## Start this weekend

Print the template or copy it into a note. Log your next trip, even if you catch nothing. Fill in the weather that evening. At the end of the month, spend 20 minutes on the review and write your two sentences. After one season, you'll know more about your home water than any pin on any map can tell you, which is also the best way to [find new spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots) that fit the same pattern.
