Minnesota's record list is more interesting than most because it's split three ways. The DNR has moved every record set without a certified scale (most of them before 1980) into a separate **non-certified** category, and reopened 11 popular species so a properly weighed fish can claim the certified title. That's why the famous 1979 walleye is still "the record" in every bait shop but sits in its own column, and why 2026 has already produced new certified records for black crappie and bluegill. The tables below come from the DNR's certified (last updated July 21, 2026), non-certified and catch-and-release lists as published in 2026, and the 2026 regulations booklet.

The DNR publishes angler names for recent records but not for most older ones, so we've only named anglers where the DNR or a news report does. The DNR does not keep line-class records. The certified and catch-and-release walleye categories were both open when we checked, which means a properly documented walleye of any qualifying size could set a record today.

## The 1979 walleye that won't go away

On May 13, 1979, LeRoy Chiovitte caught a walleye on the Seagull River at Saganaga Lake in Cook County, at the end of the Gunflint Trail: 17 lb 8 oz, 35¾ inches long, 21¼ inches around, per the DNR's listing. It has been Minnesota's walleye record for 47 years. Because it was weighed before 1980, when the DNR began requiring certified scales, it now sits in the non-certified category. It hasn't been "beaten"; the DNR simply reopened walleye as a certified-weight category, and as of the DNR's July 2026 update nobody has filled it.

What it would take: a walleye heavy enough to clear the DNR's minimum weight for the reopened category, a certified scale, a witness and a trip to a fisheries office, during the open season on public water. The Rainy River, Lake of the Woods and the big northern lakes are where the giants come from, but most anglers who catch one now release it.

## Mille Lacs muskies: both records from one lake

Mille Lacs holds both muskie records. The certified fish, 55 lb 14 oz and 57¾ inches with a 29-inch girth, was caught on November 22, 2021, in the cold last days of the season (muskie season closes November 30). Seven months later, on June 11, 2022, Eric Bakke of Princeton caught and released a 58¼-inch muskie on the same lake for the catch-and-release record. Inland, any muskie you keep must be at least 54 inches, so most big Minnesota muskies are released anyway, which is why the catch-and-release book matters as much as the scale.

## 2026: a year of panfish records

The 11 reopened species included black crappie and bluegill, and in 2026 both got certified records. David Selle's 4 lb 1 oz black crappie came from Cedar Lake in Rice County on May 3, 2026, in the spring shallow-water window. Dale Hoffman's 2 lb 3 oz bluegill, 11.5 inches long and 14¼ inches around, came from Big Stone Lake on the South Dakota border on July 7, 2026. Both are the kind of fish many anglers catch without realizing they might qualify: a big panfish needs a certified scale quickly, before it loses water weight. One wrinkle: the DNR's non-certified list as published in April 2026 still showed a 5 lb black crappie (Vermillion River, 1940) and a 2 lb 13 oz bluegill (Alice Lake, 1948), while the non-certified list in the June 2026 booklet no longer includes either. If you're chasing a panfish record, ask the DNR which marks you have to beat.

## Sturgeon: the biggest fish in the state

The heaviest certified record in Minnesota is a 94 lb 4 oz lake sturgeon from the Kettle River in Pine County (September 5, 1994), 70 inches long. The longest catch-and-release fish of any species is also a sturgeon: 80 inches from the Rainy River, caught April 18, 2026. Lake sturgeon can only be harvested on a few border waters with a $5 tag, so the release book is where new sturgeon records will come from.

## How to submit a Minnesota record fish

The DNR's rules (full package at mndnr.gov/recordfish) boil down to this:

1. **Eligibility:** caught on a public body of water, during the open season, by an angler with a valid license.
2. **Before you weigh:** check the current record and, for reopened species, the DNR's minimum weight for new submissions. Print the application and bring it.
3. **Certified weight:** weigh the fish on a state-certified scale (the DNR points to bait and butcher shops, shipping stores and grocery stores). A witness must watch the weighing and sign and date the application.
4. **Species ID:** take the fish and application to a DNR area fisheries office so a biologist can confirm the species.
5. **Photos:** show the full length of the fish on or next to a clearly marked measuring device, snout at zero and tail pinched, with clear species features.
6. **Catch-and-release records:** measure the same way and include photos or video of the fish being released unharmed; new submissions must meet minimum lengths.
7. **Deadline:** submit within 90 days of the catch. Incomplete applications are not accepted.

No new species or hybrids are being added to the record program. For technique, see [how to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish).

## How rare is a record-class Minnesota fish?

The records themselves tell you. The largemouth record has stood since 2005 at under 9 pounds; a 6-pound Minnesota largemouth is a fish most anglers never see. A 30-inch walleye is a wall-hanger anywhere in the state and still nowhere near 17 pounds.
