State records are the far end of the same urge that makes you weigh a 3-pounder at the ramp. Florida keeps its freshwater records through FWC, with weights to the hundredth of a pound, and its saltwater records through FWC's Catch a Florida Memory program with the International Game Fish Association (IGFA). This page covers both, with most of the space on fresh water, where Florida's bass record draws the questions.

## The stories behind four records

### The bass nobody can beat: 17.27 pounds, 1986

Billy O'Berry's 17.27-pound bass from Polk County has outlasted every trophy program Florida has built. FWC launched TrophyCatch in 2012, and since then anglers have documented more than 17,000 bass over 8 pounds. FWC said in 2020 that recent TrophyCatch submissions included 15- and 16-pound fish. None has been certified heavier than O'Berry's. Part of the reason is the certification rule below: a 17-pound bass photographed on a boat scale and released is a TrophyCatch Hall of Famer, not a state record.

Polk County is still a top-five county for personal-best bass in FWC's angler survey, and FWC's Florida Trophy Bass Project is trying to grow 15-pound-plus fish on purpose at Orange and Lochloosa lakes. If the record falls, those are good bets for where. See the [Florida bass guide](/fishing/florida/bass-fishing) for the program.

### The 55-year-old catfish: 73.6 pounds, 2026

Justin Hodge caught his blue catfish on the Suwannee River on February 15, 2026. According to Outdoor Life, an FWC officer met his group at the boat ramp around midnight, the fish was weighed on a certified scale at a seafood market, and a fisheries biologist confirmed the species the next morning and estimated its age at 55 to 60 years. It beat the 69.5-pound record from the Choctawhatchee River in 2015. FWC's guide lists blue and flathead catfish as nonnative, and says most nonnative fish should be harvested rather than released.

### The record that cost a fish its life: 3.93-pound spotted bass, 2025

Joe Durden of Blountstown caught an 18.25-inch spotted bass in a backwater slough off the Apalachicola River during a June 2025 tournament. His boat scale read 3 pounds 14 ounces. After weigh-in, an FWC officer witnessed it weighed at 3.93 pounds on a certified scale at a farm in Bristol, and an FWC biologist took a fin clip to confirm it was a spotted bass. It broke Dow Gilmore's 3.75-pound record from 1985. Outdoor Life reported the fish died before it could be released because of the heat and the time it took to verify. Certification takes hours. If you think you have one, keep it in a well-aerated livewell and call FWC straight away.

### The kid in the kayak: 5.95-pound shoal bass, 2017

Sheldon Grace was 14 when he caught a 5.95-pound, 22.4-inch shoal bass from a kayak on the Chipola River near Altha. It replaced a 5.20-pound fish from the Apalachicola River caught the year before. Since then, FWC has closed the Chipola to shoal bass harvest entirely; every shoal bass caught there must be released immediately. Because Chipola shoal bass can't be possessed, the next record will have to come from somewhere else in the Apalachicola system.

## How to certify a Florida freshwater state record

FWC's requirements, from its regulations guide and record announcements:

1. **Keep the fish, alive if you can.** FWC certifies records in person. For bass, an angler with a possible state record of **16.5 pounds or more** may possess it over the bag or length limit while submitting it for certification, via FWC's potential state record form. Live bass must stay on the waterbody or adjacent shore where caught, and be released only there.
2. **Call the nearest FWC regional office.** Northwest (Panama City) 850-265-3676; North Central (Lake City) 386-758-0525; Northeast (Ocala) 352-732-1225; Southwest (Lakeland) 863-648-3200; South (West Palm Beach) 561-625-5122.
3. **Weigh it on a certified scale with an FWC employee watching.** Not your boat scale. Recent records were weighed at a seafood market, a farm and an FWC hatchery; what matters is a certified scale and an FWC witness.
4. **Have an FWC biologist identify the species.** For look-alikes like spotted vs Choctaw bass, that may include a fin clip for genetics.
5. **Take photos anyway.** FWC encourages a photo on the scale with the weight visible. It won't certify the record, but it's your proof if anything goes wrong.

FWC put it bluntly in 2020: if the fish isn't certified in person by an FWC biologist, the angler won't get credit.

## Not a record? Big Catch and TrophyCatch

Most anglers will never touch a record, so FWC has two programs for fish that are just plain big.

**Big Catch** recognizes 33 freshwater species with a certificate when they meet a minimum size. Adult thresholds include:

| Species | Big Catch (adult) |
|---|---|
| Florida/largemouth bass | 24" or 8.0 lb |
| Black crappie | 14" or 2.0 lb |
| Bluegill | 10" or 1.0 lb |
| Redear sunfish | 11" or 1.25 lb |
| Channel catfish | 25" or 12.0 lb |
| Striped bass | 30" or 12.0 lb |
| Butterfly peacock bass | 18" or 4.0 lb |

Youth thresholds are lower; see BigCatchFlorida.com.

**TrophyCatch** is for bass of 8 pounds and up, photographed on a scale and released. The odds tell you how rare a true giant is: of more than 17,000 documented bass, **176 made the 13-pound Hall of Fame**, about 1%. For context on what's normal, read [what is a good size bass](/blog/what-is-a-good-size-bass) and check your fish against the [largemouth bass weight chart](/blog/largemouth-bass-weight-chart).

A Florida leaderboard on PB Fishing is a different thing again: it ranks photo-backed catches by species for the season, showing angler, state, length and weight but never the lake. It's a place for your PB, not a substitute for FWC certification.

## Florida saltwater state records

Saltwater records are run by FWC with the IGFA through Catch a Florida Memory, and they work differently:

- Categories are **All Tackle** and **Fly** weight records, plus **Youth Weight** (anglers 15 and under) and **Youth and Adult Length** records added on January 1, 2024.
- Length records cover **red drum, bonefish, permit and snook**, catch-and-release. Fish must be at least 10 inches, photographed on a flat measuring device with the marks readable (video is accepted), and each new record must beat the old one by at least half an inch.
- Applications need a notarized form signed by the angler and photos of the gear, the angler with the fish, identification views and the weight or length. Youth and length applications are due within three months of the catch, and FWC can deny an application for poor fish handling.

The first new-category records approved in 2024 included Joseph Ingold's 36.25-inch red drum, caught from a kayak near Panama City on 10-pound line, and 15-and-under angler Julia Bernstein's 20-pound, 15-ounce cobia out of Key West. Contact AnglerRecognition@MyFWC.com for the current saltwater list.
