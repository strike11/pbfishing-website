The Missouri Department of Conservation calls the walleye one of the most important freshwater game fish on the continent, and anyone who has eaten a fresh walleye fillet knows why. Walleye are also frustrating: they can shut down for days after a front, move miles between seasons, and feed hardest when you can barely see your line. This guide covers identification (including the sauger problem), the seasonal pattern, rigs with sizes and the honest numbers on size and records.

## How to identify a walleye

A walleye is a long, slender perch with two separate dorsal fins, a forked tail and a big mouth full of sharp teeth. MDC describes the back and sides as yellowish or olive-brown with darker mottling, the belly white, and the bone in front of the gill cover saw-edged. Two features matter most:

- **The eye.** Large and glassy. Walleye have a reflective layer behind the retina (the tapetum lucidum) that gathers light, which is what gives them their milky eyeshine and their low-light advantage. Wisconsin DNR notes the scientific name *vitreus* means "glassy," a reference to that eye.
- **The fins.** The spiny dorsal fin is streaked and blotched with black and usually has a large black blotch near the bases of its last few spines. The lower lobe of the tail has a white tip.

Watch the teeth and the gill-cover edge when you unhook one. Both cut.

### Walleye vs sauger vs saugeye

| Trait | Walleye | Sauger | Saugeye (hybrid) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spiny dorsal | One dark blotch at the rear base; no distinct rows of spots | Rows of distinct dark spots, no rear blotch | In between; often faint spots and a smudged blotch |
| Tail | White tip on the lower lobe | Little or no white | Some white, usually less than a walleye |
| Body | Longer, golden to olive | Smaller, slimmer, brassy with dark saddles | In between |
| Typical size | Larger | Seldom over about 3 lb | In between |

Sauger live mostly in big rivers and river reservoirs, so the confusion is worst on the Mississippi, Missouri, Ohio and Tennessee systems. Saugeye are stocked in some southern and midwestern reservoirs and can be hard to call without a lab. If you are not sure, check the dorsal fin first, and check the regulations: some states set different limits for each.

## Range and habitat

Walleye are native to Canada and the northern and central United States, including large rivers of the Mississippi drainage, and have been stocked widely beyond. Wisconsin DNR notes that early stocking blurred their original range and they are now found statewide. In Missouri they live in large streams and, through stocking, in many reservoirs.

They use open water in lakes and reservoirs and pools in rivers. MDC notes that in the Current River most walleye were in pools 12 feet or deeper with a bottom broken up by boulders or logs. Good walleye structure includes:

- Rocky points, reefs and gravel bars.
- Weed edges, especially in natural lakes.
- Mud flats with insect hatches in early summer.
- Deep basin edges and humps in midsummer.
- Current breaks below dams and in river holes.

Light runs everything. Wisconsin DNR says walleye in clear water stay deeper by day and move into the shallows at night, while in turbid water they can be caught throughout the day. On big lakes, a wind that puts a "walleye chop" on a point does the same thing as clouds.

## What walleye eat

Fry eat small crustaceans and insects. MDC notes that insects, particularly mayflies, stay important through a walleye's life, while adults feed mostly on fish: perch, shiners, shad, ciscoes, smelt and other minnows. Wisconsin DNR calls walleye "primarily minnow feeders." When a mayfly hatch hits in early summer, walleye can gorge on nymphs and ignore your minnow for a week; switching to a crawler harness or a small leech often helps.

## Life cycle and spawning

Walleye are among the first fish to spawn each spring.

- **Timing:** Wisconsin DNR says the spawning run starts soon after ice-out at water temperatures of 38–44°F and usually peaks at 42–50°F, generally mid-April to early May in Wisconsin. Missouri walleye start spawning once water passes about 46°F.
- **Where:** Gravel shoals of tributary streams, rocky points, rock reefs and the rocky faces of dams, often where wind or current keeps the rock clean.
- **How:** Walleye broadcast their eggs over rock and gravel. They are not territorial and give no parental care.
- **Fry:** Young walleye become food for many fish; the survivors become top predators that feed almost entirely on smaller fish, per MDC.

Many states protect spawning walleye with closed seasons, so spring walleye fishing often means rivers that stay open or the period right after the opener. More on that below.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Water temp | Where walleye are | What to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice-out / spawn | 38–50°F | River runs, rocky shorelines, dam faces | Jig and minnow, slow; check closed seasons |
| Late spring | 50–60°F | Shallow flats, weed edges, points | Jig and minnow, slip bobber and leech |
| Early summer | 60–70°F | Mud flats during mayfly hatches, weed edges, mid-depth structure | Crawler harness, leeches |
| Summer | 70°F+ | Deep humps, basin edges, suspended over open water; shallow at night | Bottom bouncer, trolled crankbaits, lead core |
| Fall | Cooling | Back to rocky points and shallow rock at night, following baitfish | Big minnows, jerkbaits after dark |
| Winter (ice) | Under ice | Drop-offs, humps and basin edges near feeding flats | Jigging spoons, rattle baits, deadstick minnow |

Walleye are notorious for shutting down after a cold front and bright bluebird skies. Fish deeper, slower and smaller after a front, or fish the evening of a warm, cloudy day before one. ## How to catch walleye

A 6'6"–7' medium-light spinning rod with 6–10 lb braid and a 6–8 lb fluorocarbon leader covers jigging and live bait. Trolling is easier with a longer medium rod and a line-counter reel.

1. **Jig and minnow.** A 1/8–3/8 oz round or stand-up jig with a fathead or shiner hooked through the lips. Use the lightest jig that keeps bottom contact. The classic walleye rig, spring through fall and through the ice.
2. **Live-bait (slip-sinker) rig.** A 1/4–3/4 oz walking or egg sinker, a swivel, a 3–6 ft leader and a #6–#4 hook with a leech, crawler or minnow. Drag it slowly over structure.
3. **Slip bobber.** A leech or minnow on a #6–#4 hook under a slip float set just off bottom. Deadly on rock piles and weed edges, and easy from shore.
4. **Bottom bouncer and harness.** A 1–2 oz bouncer with a 4–6 ft spinner harness and a nightcrawler, pulled at about 1 mph over flats in summer.
5. **Crankbaits.** Shallow-running stickbaits cast or trolled over rock at night in spring and fall; deeper divers trolled over basins in summer. Match the forage: perch, shad or shiner patterns.
6. **Ice.** A 1/8–1/4 oz jigging spoon or rattle bait tipped with a minnow head, with a minnow on a plain hook under a bobber as a deadstick next to it.

Shore anglers do best at night in spring and fall, casting stickbaits or jigs off rocky points, dams and piers, or fishing a slip bobber where a river enters a lake. Keep a log of what worked: walleye patterns repeat year to year on the same water, and your own notes beat any map. [What to record in a fishing log](/blog/fishing-log-what-to-record) lists what is worth writing down.

## How big do walleye get?

MDC lists adult walleye at 12–28 inches and 8 oz to 8 lb, with larger fish possible. Growth varies a lot by latitude and lake.

| Size | Approx. length | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Eater | 14–18 in, 1–2 lb | What most anglers keep |
| Good | 5 lb+, about 24 in | A solid fish in any lake |
| Excellent | 8 lb+, about 27 in | Often an old female |
| Trophy | 10 lb+, about 28–30 in | A wall fish in most states |
| World record | 25 lb | Old Hickory Lake, 1960 |

### The world record and how records are measured

Mabry Harper caught the all-tackle record walleye on Old Hickory Lake near his Tennessee home on August 2, 1960. It weighed 25 pounds. According to Game & Fish, his wife had it weighed officially at a nearby resort before it was cleaned, and the IGFA approved it quickly. Doubts grew over the years, especially about the reported 29-inch girth, and the Freshwater Fishing Hall of Fame removed it from its books in 1996. The IGFA, which holds the original documentation, still recognizes it as the heaviest walleye caught on rod and reel.

Under IGFA rules a record fish must be weighed on land on a scale certified within the past 12 months, with photos of the fish, the scale and the tackle. For a record of 25 lb or more, a new fish must beat it by one-half of one percent, which for Harper's fish works out to 2 ounces.

## Where to catch them in the pilot states

## Eating walleye

Walleye fillets are white, mild and flaky, and many anglers rank them as the best-eating freshwater fish. Keep 14–18 inch fish and release the big females, which are the spawners. Walleye are predators, and some lakes and Great Lakes waters carry consumption advice for them, often by size. Check the EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page for your state's advisory contacts. On the ice, check local ice conditions with your state agency before you walk out.

## Regulations

Walleye rules are some of the most detailed in fresh water: spring closed seasons to protect spawners, statewide openers, protected slot limits on individual lakes, special rules on border waters and the Great Lakes, and separate limits for sauger and saugeye in some states. Look up the exact lake on your state agency's site before you keep a fish.
