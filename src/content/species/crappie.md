Crappie are the fish that fill freezers and family fish fries. They school, they bite small jigs and minnows, and they are one of the few species that fish just as well from a dock in March as from a boat on a brush pile in July or through the ice in January. There are two species, the black crappie and the white crappie, and while most regulations lump them together, they prefer different water. Knowing which one you are fishing for tells you where to look.

## How to identify crappie

Both crappie are deep-bodied, slab-sided sunfish with a big, thin-lipped mouth (the "papermouth" nickname is earned: hooks tear out easily). Missouri's field guides list the shared traits: a long upper jaw reaching past the middle of the eye, a strongly concave forehead and back, a dorsal fin whose spiny and soft sections are broadly joined with no notch, and an anal fin nearly as large as the dorsal, with 6 spines.

### Black crappie vs white crappie

| Trait | Black crappie | White crappie |
|---|---|---|
| Dorsal fin spines | Usually 7 or 8 | Usually 6 |
| Pattern | Irregular black speckles and blotches all over | 5–10 faint, regular vertical bars |
| Body shape | Deeper, heavier for its length | Slightly longer and slimmer |
| Water | Clearer water, vegetation, less tolerant of mud | Handles turbid water and silt better |
| Usual max size (Missouri) | Seldom over 14 in | Seldom over 15 in |

The spine count is the reliable test, because color fades fast on a stringer and spawning males of both species turn dark. Hold the fish, raise the dorsal fin with your thumb and count the hard spines from the front. Six means white; seven or eight means black.

Hybrids happen where both species live, and they can carry in-between counts and patterns. That is why the Tennessee black crappie that set the current world record was DNA-tested before it was certified.

## Range and habitat

Crappie are native to eastern and central North America and have been stocked across the country. White crappie are the more abundant species in many southern and midwestern reservoirs; MDC says black crappie are "widespread but sporadic" in Missouri and most common in large Ozark reservoirs, Mississippi River pools and natural lakes. In the north, black crappie dominate the weedy natural lakes.

Both species live in open water near cover: standing timber, brush piles, bridge pilings, docks and weed edges in ponds, lakes, reservoirs and the slow backwaters of big rivers. Black crappie are less tolerant of turbid water and siltation, MDC notes, so in a muddy reservoir expect mostly whites. Wisconsin DNR says crappie hold in weed beds in deep water by day and bite best in early morning and toward evening; MDC adds that white crappie are most active in the evening and at night. That is why lighted docks and night fishing under lanterns work so well in summer.

## What crappie eat

Small fish, mainly minnows and young shad, plus aquatic insects and small crustaceans, according to MDC. Crappie feed by looking up and ambushing prey above them, so a bait held slightly above the school beats one dragged through it. Wisconsin DNR notes that in summer, when young-of-year fish are everywhere, crappie are harder to catch: they have plenty of real food.

## Life cycle and spawning

Crappie are among the first sunfish to move shallow in spring.

- **Timing:** Both species spawn from about mid-April to early June in Missouri, once water passes about 56°F. Wisconsin DNR puts favorable black crappie spawning temperatures at 64–68°F, usually in May and June, delayed to July in cold years. In Texas and Florida it starts much earlier, often in late winter.
- **Where:** Coves protected from wave action, with silt-free bottom, usually near plants or submerged wood. Males sweep nests in sand or fine gravel.
- **Colonies:** White crappie nest in colonies; MDC records up to 35 nests in one colony. Find one fish and you have often found 20.
- **Guarding:** The male guards the nest and defends the young until they start feeding.
- **Lifespan:** Short. Most crappie live 3–4 years, occasionally 8 or more. Black crappie grow slower in length than whites but weigh more at any given length.

Short lives and huge spawns make crappie populations boom and bust. A lake full of 8-inch fish this year can be full of 11-inchers in two years and thin the year after, which is why your local reports matter more than last year's memories.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Water temp | Where crappie are | How to fish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter | Below 45°F | Suspended over deep basins and channel edges, often 15–30 ft | Vertical jig or minnow, very slow; through the ice in the north |
| Prespawn | 45–55°F | Staging on creek channels and brush leading to coves | Long-line trolling jigs, slip-float minnows |
| Spawn | ~56–68°F | Shallow coves, 1–6 ft, around wood, reeds and docks | Jig under a float, cast and swim small jigs |
| Postspawn | 68–75°F | Pulling back to the first brush piles and docks | Shoot docks, fish brush piles 8–15 ft |
| Summer | 75°F+ | Deep brush, bridge pilings, suspended near shade; shallow at night | Vertical jig brush; lights at night |
| Fall | Cooling | Following shad into creeks, then back to deep brush | Trolling, casting small swimbaits |

The spawn is the easiest time to catch a limit from the bank, and the [best time to fish](/blog/best-time-to-fish) guide explains how to pick the day. Cold fronts push crappie deeper and tighter to cover. After a front, downsize and slow down.

## How to catch crappie

Light gear: a 5'6"–7' ultralight or light spinning rod with 4–6 lb line, or a 10–14 ft jig pole for dipping cover. Here is what works:

1. **Jig and plastic.** A 1/32–1/8 oz jig head with a 1.5–2.5 inch tube, curly tail or minnow body. Use 1/32–1/16 oz shallow and 1/8 oz in deeper water or wind. Chartreuse and white in stained water; smoke, pearl or shad colors in clear water; black and chartreuse at night.
2. **Minnow under a float.** A small minnow hooked through the lips or back on a #4–#6 light-wire hook, a split shot and a slip float set just above the depth of the fish. The best single rig for beginners. (See [fishing for beginners](/blog/fishing-for-beginners) for a starter setup.)
3. **Jig under a float.** A 1/32–1/16 oz jig 1–3 ft under a float, twitched over spawning cover. Wisconsin DNR's advice applies: keep the bait moving.
4. **Vertical jigging.** Drop a jig or minnow straight down into a brush pile or along a bridge piling, and hold it just above the fish.
5. **Dock shooting.** Skip a 1/16 oz jig deep under a dock with a slingshot cast in summer and postspawn.
6. **Trolling.** Long-lining or "spider rigging" several rods with jigs at 0.5–1.0 mph covers prespawn and fall water fast.

Set the hook gently and play the fish with a soft rod. Crappie mouths are thin, and Wisconsin DNR specifically warns to land them carefully. In winter, small jigging spoons and plastics on 2–4 lb line through the ice catch suspended crappie across Minnesota and Michigan.

Forward-facing sonar has changed crappie fishing on many reservoirs, letting anglers target individual big fish in open water. If you do not have it, fish brush piles and docks, which still hold plenty.

## How big do crappie get?

MDC puts both species at 9–10 inches in usual total length, with whites seldom over 15 inches and blacks seldom over 14 inches.

| Size | Approx. length | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Keeper | 9–10 in | Typical eating size; check local minimum lengths |
| Good | 12 in, about 1 lb | A solid fish for the fryer |
| Slab | 14 in+, 1.5–2 lb | Bragging size anywhere |
| Trophy | 3 lb+ | Very rare; most anglers never see one |
| World record | 5 lb 7 oz (black), 5 lb 3 oz (white) | Ferguson 2018, Bright 1957 |

### The world records and how they are measured

Fred Bright's white crappie, caught July 31, 1957, at Enid Dam in Mississippi, weighed 5 lb 3 oz and reportedly measured 21 inches with a 19-inch girth. Outdoor Life notes there are no known photos of the fish, and it is still the all-tackle record.

The black crappie record is much newer. Lionel "Jam" Ferguson caught a 5 lb 7 oz black crappie on a jig in a private pond in Tennessee on May 15, 2018. It measured 19.25 inches with a 17.75-inch girth. The scale read 5 lb 7.86 oz; the IGFA lists it at 5 lb 7 oz. DNA testing confirmed it was a pure black crappie, not a hybrid. It replaced a 5-pound fish from a private Missouri lake in 2006.

IGFA all-tackle records are by weight on a scale certified in the past 12 months, weighed on land, with photos of the fish, the scale and the tackle. A new record under 25 lb must beat the old one by 2 ounces. State records follow each agency's rules; many require an agency biologist to confirm the species, which for crappie can mean counting spines or a genetic test.

For your own slabs, a length on a board and a photo do most of the work; see [how to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish). ## Eating crappie

Crappie are among the best-tasting freshwater fish: white, flaky and mild. Fillet them, rinse, and fry or bake; small ones can be scaled and cooked whole. Because they live short lives and mostly eat small prey, crappie are often a safer choice than big, old predators, but advisories are set water by water. Check the EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page for your state's advisory contacts before you keep fish from a new lake.

## Regulations

Crappie limits are among the most variable in fresh water. Statewide creel limits, minimum lengths and lake-specific rules all differ, and a cooler full of crappie is easy to overfill without noticing. Count as you go, keep each angler's fish separate, and check the rules for the exact water on your state agency's site. Your state page is a good start, for example [Tennessee fishing license](/fishing/tennessee/fishing-license) or [Texas fishing license](/fishing/texas/fishing-license).
