The channel catfish is the fish a lot of Americans caught first, on a worm under a bobber at a city pond, and the one a lot of them still fish for on summer nights with a lawn chair and a lantern. Texas Parks and Wildlife says channel cats rank behind only bass and crappie as the fish Texas anglers prefer. They live in almost every kind of warm water, they eat almost anything, and they fight hard for their size. This guide covers identification (including blue and flathead look-alikes), habits, rigs and the honest size numbers.

## How to identify a channel catfish

A channel catfish has a long, scaleless body, eight barbels ("whiskers") around the mouth, a sharp spine at the front of the dorsal and pectoral fins, and a small fleshy adipose fin on the back. The Missouri Department of Conservation and TPWD list the details that separate it from other catfish:

- **Tail:** deeply forked. Among common catfish only the blue cat shares this.
- **Spots:** scattered dark spots on the back and sides. They are often missing on the smallest young and on big adults.
- **Anal fin:** outer edge rounded outward, usually 24–29 rays, with a fairly short base.
- **Profile:** the back slopes gently, so the head is less wedge-shaped than a blue cat's.
- **Jaw:** the upper jaw sticks out past the lower jaw, unlike a flathead.
- **Color:** olive-brown to slate-blue back and a silvery-white belly. Breeding males turn deep blue-black with swollen heads and thick lips, which fools a lot of people into calling them blues.

### Channel vs blue vs flathead catfish

| Trait | Channel catfish | Blue catfish | Flathead catfish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tail | Deeply forked | Deeply forked | Square or slightly notched |
| Spots | Usually present (not on big adults) | Never | Mottled brown-yellow, no round spots |
| Anal fin | Rounded edge, 24–29 rays | Straight edge, 30–35 rays | Short, rounded |
| Head | Moderate slope | Wedge-shaped, humped back | Wide, flat; lower jaw sticks out |
| Size | Commonly to 15 lb | Grows much larger (record 143 lb) | Grows much larger (record 123 lb) |

The anal-fin check works on any size fish: lay the fish flat, look at the long fin along the belly, and see whether the bottom edge is curved (channel) or straight like it was cut with a ruler (blue). If a photo-ID app disagrees with what you see, trust the fin. Anglers on r/FishingForBeginners joke about an app that "tagged my dog as a flathead catfish," and a one-second fin check is still the fastest way to settle it.

## Range and habitat

Channel catfish are native to North America east of the Rockies and have been stocked widely elsewhere, including as far west as California, per TPWD. TPWD believes they are not native to the upper Rio Grande and Pecos basins in Texas, though they are there now.

MDC describes them as fish of large, fairly turbid streams with low to moderate current, and of reservoirs and ponds. Adults rest by day in deep pools, holes and around cover such as submerged logs; young fish use riffles and shallow pool edges. At night adults move into riffles and shallows to feed. That daily movement is the whole game:

- **Rivers:** fish the deep outside bends, log jams and holes by day; the riffles, heads of pools and shallow flats at night.
- **Reservoirs:** creek channels, points and flats near deep water; windblown banks; the mouths of feeder creeks after rain.
- **Ponds and small lakes:** the deepest water by day, the shallow ends and any inflow at night. Many city and park ponds are stocked with channel cats, which makes them the easiest catfish for bank anglers.

## What channel catfish eat

They are omnivores. MDC lists fish, insects, crayfish, mollusks and plant material, and notes a Des Moines River study that found insects from 50 different families in channel cat stomachs. Fish under 4 inches eat almost only small insects; bigger fish eat bigger and more varied food, with fish and crayfish becoming more important.

Channel cats hunt with their barbels and skin, which carry taste receptors, plus a strong sense of smell. That is why scent matters more than color for catfish, and why fresh bait outfishes old bait.

## Life cycle and spawning

- **Timing:** Spawning starts when water reaches about 75°F. In Missouri that is roughly the last week of May through the third week of July, often with two peaks. Texas spawns earlier.
- **Nest:** The male picks and cleans a dark, sheltered cavity: drift piles, hollow logs, undercut banks, rocks, muskrat or beaver burrows. (This is why fisheries managers sink "catfish boxes" in ponds.)
- **Eggs:** The female lays a golden-yellow, gelatinous egg mass on the nest floor. The male guards it and fans it. TPWD notes he may eat eggs if disturbed.
- **Fry:** Eggs hatch in about a week; fry stay in the nest another 7–8 days. Young survive better in muddy water than clear, where predators pick them off.
- **Growth:** Missouri channel cats mature at about 4–5 years and 12–15 inches; Texas populations mature at 3–6 years. Most live 6–7 years, a few past 10.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Water temp | Where to fish | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early spring | 50–60°F | Shallow flats and coves that warm first; inflows after rain | Cut bait; the first warm rains start the bite |
| Prespawn | 60–75°F | Riprap, rocky banks, creek mouths | Often the best numbers of the year |
| Spawn | ~75°F+ | Males in cavities; others still feeding | Fish around rock and wood |
| Summer | 80°F+ | Deep holes by day, shallows and riffles at night | Night fishing shines |
| Fall | Cooling | Following shad into creeks and onto flats | Fresh cut shad |
| Winter | Below 50°F | Deep holes, slow water | Slower, smaller baits; still catchable |

Rising water in rivers, especially the first day or two after a rain that brings fresh food downstream, is a classic catfish trigger. See [fishing after rain, wind and a cold front](/blog/fishing-after-rain-wind-cold-front).

## How to catch channel catfish

A 7–8 ft medium or medium-heavy rod with 12–20 lb mono handles most channel cat fishing. Use circle hooks for any bait fished on a still rod: the fish hooks itself in the corner of the mouth, which makes release easy.

1. **Slip-sinker rig (Carolina or "Santee" rig).** A 1/2–2 oz egg or no-roll sinker on the main line, a bead, a swivel, then a 12–18 inch leader to a 2/0–5/0 circle hook. Add a small peg float on the leader (the Santee version) to lift bait off snags. The default rig for bank fishing.
2. **Bobber rig.** A float set so a worm or liver hangs just off the bottom, with a #2–1/0 hook. Great for ponds and kids.
3. **Three-way rig.** A 1–3 oz bell sinker on a short dropper and an 18–24 inch leader to the hook. Holds bait in river current below dams and riffles.
4. **Dip or punch bait.** A #6–#2 treble hook inside a ribbed dip worm or a sponge, packed with prepared bait. Best in summer in ponds and rivers.

Baits, roughly in order of how often they work:

- **Cut shad, skipjack or bluegill** (where legal), fresh and bloody. Big channel cats love it.
- **Nightcrawlers**, a gob on the hook.
- **Chicken liver**, tied on with thread or cured with salt so it stays put.
- **Shrimp, hot dogs, cheese baits, stinkbait.** TPWD lists liver, worms, grasshoppers, shrimp, chicken, cheese and stinkbait.

Cast to the edges, not the middle: the drop-off at the end of a flat, the seam below a riffle, the downstream side of a log jam. Give a spot 20–30 minutes, then move. Set the rod in a holder with the reel's clicker or a loose drag, and with a circle hook, just reel steadily when the rod loads. Do not jerk.

## How big do channel catfish get?

MDC puts typical channel cats at 12–32 inches and 1–15 lb, with 45-pounders uncommon in Missouri. TPWD says Texas waters have produced channel cats over 36 lb.

| Size | Approx. length | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Fiddler | Under 14 in, under 1 lb | Great eating, most of a pond catch |
| Average | 15–22 in, 1–5 lb | A normal evening |
| Good | 10 lb+, about 28 in | A heavy fish that will pull drag |
| Trophy | 20 lb+ | A big channel cat in any state |
| World record | 58 lb | Santee-Cooper, 1964 |

### The world record and how catfish are measured

W.H. Whaley's 58-pound channel cat from South Carolina's Santee-Cooper Reservoir, caught in July 1964, is still the IGFA all-tackle record. In-Fisherman has pointed out that clear photos of it are rare, that the few seen show a tail "less deeply forked" than a channel cat's, and that no channel cat caught in modern times comes close, which has led some to wonder if it was a blue. The record stands.

For comparison, the blue catfish benchmark is Nick Anderson's 143 lb fish from Virginia's Kerr Reservoir in 2011, and the flathead is Ken Paulie's 123 lb fish from Elk City Reservoir, Kansas, in 1998.

IGFA records are by weight on a scale certified within 12 months, weighed on land, with photos of the fish, scale and tackle. For a record under 25 lb, a new fish must beat the old one by 2 ounces; at 25 lb and up the margin is one-half of one percent.

For your own PB: measure total length with the mouth closed and the tail lobes pinched together, and weigh the fish in a sling or wet net, then subtract the sling. [How to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish) has the details, and the [fish weight calculator](/tools/fish-weight-calculator) estimates weight from length (or length and girth) when you had no scale. ## Eating channel catfish

TPWD says channel cats are prized for their flavor, and 1–5 lb fish are the best eating: firm, white and sweet. Skin them, trim the fat and dark red meat along the lateral line, and fry in cornmeal. Advisories are set water by water and are often species-specific, and catfish from some rivers appear on them, so check before you keep fish from an unfamiliar water. The EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page links to every state's advisory contacts.

## Regulations

Catfish rules differ by state and often by species: some states combine channel and blue catfish in one daily bag, some have separate limits, and some lakes have length limits that protect big fish. Rules on using live bait, game fish as bait, and the number of lines or unattended rods also vary. Check the exact water on your state agency's site before you go, starting with your state's license page, for example [Texas](/fishing/texas/fishing-license) or [Tennessee](/fishing/tennessee/fishing-license).
