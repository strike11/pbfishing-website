Hook a 3-pound smallmouth on light line and you will understand why northern anglers call it the best fight in fresh water. Wisconsin's Department of Natural Resources calls it, pound for pound, the scrappiest fish in the state. Smallmouth live in different places from largemouth, eat different things and react to weather differently, so treating them like a green bass with a brown coat costs you fish. This guide covers identification, the seasonal pattern, rigs that work and the honest numbers on size.

## How to identify a smallmouth bass

A smallmouth is a long, muscular bass with a moderately large mouth. The back and sides are greenish brown to bronze with faint dark mottling and a series of separate vertical bars; the belly is whitish. Missouri's field guide lists the details that matter:

- The upper jaw reaches about the rear margin of the eye in adults and does not extend past it.
- There is no dark horizontal stripe and no rows of dark spots.
- The two dorsal fins are connected.
- Cheek scales are much smaller than body scales.
- The tongue usually has a rough patch.
- Eyes often look red to orange, and many fish show dark bars radiating back from the eye.

Bar intensity changes with mood and water clarity. A fish pulled from deep, clear water can look almost gold; one from a tannic river can look dark brown. Females entering the nest develop a strongly barred and blotched pattern, while males keep uniform color, per MDC.

### Smallmouth vs largemouth vs spotted bass

| Trait | Smallmouth | Largemouth | Spotted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper jaw | Ends around the rear of the eye | Far past the rear of the eye | To, or just past, the rear of the eye |
| Side pattern | Vertical bars, no stripe | Broad dark horizontal stripe | Stripe plus rows of spots on the lower side |
| Color | Bronze, brown, olive | Green | Green |
| Dorsal fins | Connected | Barely connected | Connected |

Spotted bass cause the most confusion in Tennessee and the Ozarks. The quick test is the side: a spotted bass has a horizontal stripe with wavy edges and neat rows of dark dots under it. A smallmouth has vertical bars and no stripe. Hybrids exist, so if a fish shows a bit of both, log it as what the agency would call it and note the doubt.

## Range and habitat

Smallmouth are native to the Great Lakes and the Mississippi, Ohio and Tennessee river systems, and have been stocked widely beyond. MDC describes them as fish of cool, clear streams with silt-free rock or gravel bottoms, near riffles but out of the main current, and of large clear reservoirs. In the north they also live in natural lakes. Wisconsin finds them in medium to large streams and large clear-water lakes, "usually associated with rocky" environments.

If you are reading a lake map, smallmouth water looks like this:

- Rock: boulders, rip-rap, rocky points, gravel bars and humps.
- Clarity: you can often see the bottom in 6–10 feet.
- Current or wind: river eddies, current seams below riffles, windblown points on big lakes.
- Depth nearby: smallies push shallow to feed and drop back to deep water fast.

They are most active at dawn and dusk, according to MDC, and on big northern lakes they often feed hardest when the wind is up.

## What smallmouth bass eat

Crayfish come first. Wisconsin DNR calls crayfish their favorite food, and anyone who has cleaned a smallmouth has seen claws in the stomach. Minnows, sculpins, gobies in the Great Lakes, hellgrammites, dragonfly larvae and other big aquatic insects make up the rest. MDC describes adults following a digging turtle or sucker to pick off the crayfish and insects it stirs up, which tells you how opportunistic they are.

That diet explains the tackle box: brown, green-pumpkin and orange craw colors near the bottom, and goby or shad-colored minnow baits when they suspend.

## Life cycle and spawning

Smallmouth spawn earlier and cooler than most people think.

- **Temperature:** Wisconsin DNR says spawning usually occurs at 62–64°F, with spawning seen as low as 53°F. In southern Wisconsin it runs mid-May through June in water from 55 to 75°F. In Missouri it begins in early or mid-April once water passes 60°F, peaks in May and can stretch into June or July.
- **Nest:** The male sweeps a round, saucer-shaped nest about twice his body length across, floored with coarse gravel and rock, in water with no perceptible current. In rivers that means behind a boulder or log; in lakes, protected gravel flats near deeper water. Males may build several "practice nests" first.
- **Eggs:** Nests average about 2,500 eggs and can hold 10,000. Several females may use one nest.
- **Fry:** Eggs hatch in 2–3 days. Fry stay over the nest about 12 more days while the male guards them. MDC notes that one mile of Ozark stream can produce 80,000 fry, which can drop to under 100 by early September.
- **Lifespan:** about 10–12 years.

That survival number is the strongest argument for going easy on bedding fish. A male pulled off his nest for a photo and a long weigh-in can come back to an empty bed.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Water temp | Where smallmouth are | What to throw |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early spring | 45–55°F | Deep breaks next to spawning flats, river wintering holes | Suspending jerkbait, hair jig, blade bait |
| Prespawn | 55–60°F | Gravel flats, rocky points, 4–12 ft | Jerkbait, tube, swimbait |
| Spawn | ~60–65°F | Gravel beds in protected water | Tube, Ned rig, sight fishing (where legal) |
| Summer | 68°F+ | Offshore humps, rock piles, current seams; shallow at dawn | Drop shot, topwater, crayfish imitations |
| Fall | Cooling | Chasing baitfish on points and flats, then dropping deeper | Swimbait, jerkbait, blade bait |
| Winter | Below 45°F | Deep, slow water | Hair jig, slow drop shot, float-and-fly |

On big northern lakes, fall is the time to chase the biggest fish of the year: they feed heavily before winter. Wisconsin's DNR says the best lake fishing comes in June, during and just after spawning, and in early fall.

## How to catch smallmouth bass

Light line and patience beat heavy gear. A 6'10"–7'2" medium-light or medium spinning rod with 8–10 lb braid and a 6–8 lb fluorocarbon leader handles most of it.

1. **Tube.** A 3.5-inch tube on a 1/8–3/8 oz internal jig head, dragged and hopped over rock. Green pumpkin, brown or smoke. It looks like a fleeing crayfish or goby, and it has caught more big smallmouth than any other bait.
2. **Ned rig.** A 2.75–3 inch floating stick bait on a 1/10–1/5 oz mushroom head. The answer on post-front days and in pressured water.
3. **Drop shot.** A 3–4 inch minnow or finesse worm on a #1 or #2 hook, 12–18 inches above a 1/4–3/8 oz weight. Use it for suspended fish you can see on sonar in summer.
4. **Suspending jerkbait.** 4–4.5 inch, in cold clear water from 45 to 55°F. Twitch, twitch, long pause.
5. **Topwater.** Walking baits and poppers on calm summer mornings and over shallow gravel in fall.
6. **Swimbait or hair jig.** A 3–4 inch paddle-tail on a 1/4–3/8 oz head for fall chasing fish; a 1/8–1/4 oz hair jig crawled slowly in winter.

Natural bait works: a crayfish, hellgrammite or minnow on a #2 hook with a split shot, drifted along rock. Wisconsin DNR suggests early morning and late evening for natural baits, and its advice for wading streams holds anywhere: fish quietly, cast toward rocks or logs, keep the rod tip up and the line tight.

Smallmouth jump. When one heads for the surface, keep the rod low and the line tight so the hook does not slip on the head shake.

## How big do smallmouth bass get?

MDC lists usual adults at 10–20 inches and ½–4 lb, with a maximum around 22 inches and 6 lb in Missouri. Growth depends heavily on latitude, food and season length.

| Size | Approx. length | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Average | 12–15 in, 1–2 lb | A normal day on a good river |
| Good | 3 lb+, about 17 in | Worth a photo |
| Excellent | 4 lb+, about 18–19 in | The fish people remember |
| Trophy | 5 lb+, about 20 in | Rare almost everywhere |
| World record | 11 lb 15 oz | Dale Hollow, 1955 |

### The Dale Hollow record and how records are measured

David Hayes caught the all-tackle world record on July 9, 1955, while trolling on Dale Hollow Lake, which straddles the Tennessee–Kentucky line. It weighed 11 lb 15 oz. In 1996 the IGFA struck the record after a sworn affidavit claimed the catch was a hoax, and handed it to John Gorman's 10 lb 14 oz fish from the same lake, caught in 1969. After further review the IGFA reinstated Hayes in December 2005. The two biggest smallmouth on record both came from Dale Hollow.

Under IGFA rules a new all-tackle record must be weighed on land on a scale certified in the past 12 months and, for fish under 25 lb, beat the old mark by at least 2 ounces. The application needs photos of the fish, the scale and the tackle. Length records are separate and only for released fish.

For your own records, measure total length on a flat board with the mouth closed and the tail pinched, and photograph the fish side-on on the board. ## Eating smallmouth bass

Smallmouth from cold, clean water are firm and mild. Most smallmouth anglers release them anyway, because big ones are slow to replace. If you keep a couple, take fish in the 12–15 inch range and check advisories first: Great Lakes and some river fish carry state-issued limits. The EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page links to every state's advisory contacts.

## Regulations

Smallmouth rules vary a lot by state and by water: minimum lengths, slot limits, and in several northern states a spring season that is catch-and-release only or closed during the spawn. Bait rules matter too. Wisconsin prohibits possessing live crayfish while fishing inland waters (the Mississippi River excepted), which surprises visiting anglers who brought a bucket from home. Check the rules for the exact water on your state's agency site before you keep a fish, starting from your state page such as [Michigan](/fishing/michigan/fishing-license) or [Minnesota](/fishing/minnesota/fishing-license). Releasing a fish fast is the best thing you can do for a smallmouth fishery; see [how to release a fish](/blog/how-to-release-a-fish).
