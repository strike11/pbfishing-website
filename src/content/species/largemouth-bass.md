If you fish fresh water in the United States, you have probably caught a largemouth bass, and you have definitely seen one argued over. It is the fish behind most "new PB" posts, most tournament checks and most of the "no scale, guess the weight" threads on r/bassfishing. This guide covers the biology that actually helps you catch one: how to tell it from the other black basses, where it lives by season, what triggers the spawn, and what size really counts as big.

## How to identify a largemouth bass

A largemouth is a long, heavy-bodied sunfish with a huge mouth. The back is green to olive, the belly white to pale green, and a row of dark blotches usually runs down the side as a broad horizontal stripe. Texas Parks and Wildlife lists the key details: the upper jaw "reaches far beyond the rear margin of the eye," and the dorsal fin is nearly divided, with nine spines in front and 12–13 soft rays behind.

That jaw is the fastest field check. Close the fish's mouth and look at where the corner of the jaw ends. If it reaches well behind the eye, you have a largemouth. Small juveniles are the exception, because their jaws are still catching up.

### Largemouth vs spotted vs smallmouth bass

The three black basses overlap across much of the South and Midwest, and they hybridize in some lakes, so check more than one trait. The Missouri Department of Conservation field guides give these differences:

| Trait | Largemouth | Spotted | Smallmouth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper jaw | Reaches far past the rear of the eye | Reaches to, or just past, the rear of the eye | Reaches about the rear margin of the eye |
| Dorsal fins | Barely connected, deep notch | Connected | Connected |
| Tongue | Smooth | Rough tooth patch | Usually a rough patch |
| Side pattern | One broad dark stripe, no rows of spots below it | Stripe plus rows of small dark spots on the lower side | Vertical bars, no stripe |
| Color | Green to olive | Green with a wavy-edged stripe | Bronze to brown |

The spotted bass check that settles most arguments: run a finger over the tongue. A rough patch means spotted (or smallmouth); smooth means largemouth.

### A note on the name

In June 2024 the International Game Fish Association adopted newly published research that treats the Florida bass as its own species. The IGFA now uses *Micropterus nigricans* for the largemouth and *Micropterus salmoides* for the Florida bass, and notes the two are often indistinguishable without a genetic test. Most state agencies still lump them as "largemouth bass" in their regulations, and the IGFA now keeps a combined largemouth/Florida category for line-class and length records. For your fishing day, nothing changes. For a new all-tackle world record claim, it does: the IGFA says any new all-tackle submission will need genetic verification.

## Range and habitat

Largemouth bass are native to most of the United States east of the Rocky Mountains, with fringe populations in southeastern Canada and northeastern Mexico. They have been stocked into nearly every state and across Central and South America, Europe and Japan. Florida-strain fish have been widely introduced into Texas and other southern states to grow bigger bass.

They like warm, fairly clear water with little or no current: natural lakes, reservoirs, farm ponds, river backwaters and the slow pools of larger streams. What they want most is cover. TPWD lists logs, rock ledges, vegetation and docks; in practice that means grass lines, laydowns, stumps, boat docks, brush piles and the edges where one kind of cover meets another. MDC notes they are most active at dawn and dusk, which is why early and late trips outfish midday ones on hot, clear days.

## What largemouth bass eat

Fry start on zooplankton and insect larvae. Once they reach about two inches they turn into predators, and adults "feed almost exclusively on other fish and large invertebrates such as crayfish," per TPWD. Shad, bluegill, shiners, small bass, crayfish, frogs and anything that falls in the water (MDC lists mice and snakes) are on the menu.

Match your lure to what the local bass are eating. In shad reservoirs that means silver and white baits that swim. In grassy natural lakes, think bluegill colors, craws and frogs. In a farm pond full of stunted sunfish, a green-pumpkin jig or a bluegill-pattern swimbait is hard to beat.

## Life cycle and spawning

Spawning starts in spring when shallow water reaches about 60°F. In Texas that can be as early as February or as late as May; in Missouri it runs mid-April through June, and farther north it slides into June. Expect a few warm days to push fish shallow and a cold front to pull them back.

- **Nests:** Males fan out a bed in 2–8 feet of water (MDC records nests from under a foot to 15 feet), on any firm bottom except soft mud: sand, gravel, roots, even a sunken log. They avoid current and wave action, so look in protected pockets, coves and the back sides of grass.
- **Eggs:** A female lays 2,000 to 43,000 eggs, then the male chases her off and guards the nest. Eggs hatch in about a week, sometimes faster in warm water.
- **Fry:** The male guards the school of fry for days to weeks; MDC calls the largemouth male the most attentive parent of any sunfish.
- **Growth:** TPWD gives 4–6 inches after year one, 8–12 inches after year two and about 16 inches at three years in Texas. Northern bass grow slower.
- **Lifespan:** 10–15 years in Missouri, 16 on average per TPWD. Every big bass is an old bass.

That last point matters. A 10-pounder has survived a decade of herons, droughts and hooks. If you want more of them in your lake, release the big females.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Water temp | Where bass are | What to throw |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late winter / prespawn | 48–58°F | Points and channel swings leading into spawning flats | Lipless crankbait, jerkbait, jig |
| Spawn | ~60–68°F | Protected pockets, 1–8 ft | Soft-plastic creature bait, wacky worm, sight fishing |
| Postspawn | 68–75°F | First breaks outside the spawning areas, docks, shad spawns | Topwater at dawn, swim jig, fluke |
| Summer | 75°F+ | Offshore ledges, deep grass edges, shade | Big worm, deep crankbait, frog in the mats |
| Fall | Cooling from 75°F to 55°F | Back into creeks chasing shad | Spinnerbait, squarebill, topwater |
| Winter | Below 50°F | Deep, steep structure near food | Slow jig, blade bait, drop shot |

Temperature drives most of this. If you want the reasoning, see [water temperature and fish activity](/blog/water-temperature-and-fish-activity) and the season-by-season lure picks in [best bass lures by season](/blog/best-bass-lures-by-season).

## How to catch largemouth bass

You do not need 30 rods. These six setups cover most water:

1. **Texas-rigged soft plastic.** A 7–10 inch worm or a 4–5 inch creature bait on a 3/0–5/0 offset hook, with a 1/8–3/8 oz bullet weight (go heavier to punch grass). Drag it through brush and grass on 15–20 lb fluorocarbon. Green pumpkin in clear water, junebug or black-blue in stain.
2. **Wacky-rigged stick bait.** A 5-inch stick worm hooked through the middle on a #1 or 1/0 hook, no weight. Cast it to docks and spawning cover and let it fall. Little skill required, and it is the first rig to try if you are [not catching fish](/blog/why-am-i-not-catching-fish).
3. **Jig.** A 3/8–1/2 oz flipping or football jig with a craw trailer. It catches the biggest fish in most lakes because it looks like a crayfish.
4. **Spinnerbait or bladed jig.** 3/8–1/2 oz, white or chartreuse-white in stained water. Cover water fast on windy banks and grass edges.
5. **Crankbaits.** A 1/2 oz lipless bait in late winter, a squarebill for shallow wood, a deep diver for summer ledges. Red and crawfish colors in early spring, shad colors the rest of the year.
6. **Topwater.** A walking bait or popper at dawn, a hollow-body frog over matted vegetation in summer. Wait until you feel the weight before setting the hook.

Live bait works too: a 4–6 inch golden shiner under a float is how a lot of Florida trophies get caught. Fish it on a 3/0–5/0 circle hook so the fish is hooked in the jaw and can be released.

From the bank, work the shoreline with a weightless worm and a spinnerbait, and look for the spots others skip: culverts, the windy side, outside bends with fallen trees. For finding public water close to home, see [how to find fishing spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots).

## How big do largemouth bass get?

MDC puts typical adults at 10–20 inches and ½–4½ lb. Here is how anglers generally grade them:

| Size | Length (approx.) | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Dink | Under 12 in | Most of what you catch in a pressured pond |
| Keeper / average | 12–17 in, 1–3 lb | A normal day |
| Good bass | 5 lb+, about 20 in | A fish worth a photo in any state |
| Lunker | 8 lb+ or 24 in | ShareLunker's entry level in Texas |
| Double-digit | 10 lb+ | A real trophy, mostly in southern and Florida-strain water |
| World record | 22 lb 4 oz | Perry (1932) and Kurita (2009) |

Length-to-weight varies with season and body shape; a prespawn female carries far more weight than the same fish in August. The [largemouth bass weight chart](/blog/largemouth-bass-weight-chart) and the [fish weight calculator](/tools/fish-weight-calculator) give honest estimates when you had no scale, and [what is a good size bass](/blog/what-is-a-good-size-bass) goes deeper on rarity.

### The world record and how records are measured

George Perry's bass from Montgomery Lake, Georgia, on June 2, 1932, weighed 22 lb 4 oz and stood alone for 77 years. In 2009 Manabu Kurita caught a Lake Biwa, Japan, bass reported at 22 lb 4.97 oz. IGFA rules say a record under 25 pounds must be beaten by at least 2 ounces, so the IGFA certified Kurita as a co-holder rather than a new record.

IGFA all-tackle records are by weight. The fish must be weighed on land on a scale certified within the past 12 months, any weight between two graduations is rounded down, and the application needs photos of the full fish, the scale and the rod and reel. Separate all-tackle length records exist for fish released alive. State records follow their own agency rules, usually a certified scale, witnesses and an agency biologist confirming the species.

Most of us will never chase a record, but a PB is a record too, and the same habits apply: a measured length, a weight on a real scale and a clear side-on photo. As anglers on r/bassfishing put it, "bass people go by weight." [How to measure a fish](/blog/how-to-measure-a-fish) covers the details. ## Eating largemouth bass

Largemouth are good eating at 12–15 inches: white, mild fillets that fry well. Fish from warm, muddy ponds in summer can taste weedy; skinning the fillets and trimming the dark lateral line helps. Bigger bass are older, and mercury builds up in predators as they age, so the small ones are the better meal and the big ones are the better release.

States, tribes and territories publish fish consumption advisories for specific waters. Before you keep fish from a new lake, check the EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page, which links to every state's advisory contacts.

## Regulations

Bass rules vary more than most: statewide minimum lengths, slot limits on individual lakes, different bag limits for largemouth and spotted bass, and in some northern states a catch-and-release-only season during the spawn. Check the rules for the exact water before you keep a fish, and handle spawning bass quickly so the male gets back to his nest. Start with your state's page, for example the [Texas](/fishing/texas/fishing-license) or [Florida](/fishing/florida/fishing-license) license guides, then the agency's lake-specific exceptions.
