Rainbow trout are the fish most people mean when they say "trout." State and federal hatcheries raise them by the hundreds of thousands, so they swim in city ponds in winter, mountain streams in summer and cold tailwaters below dams all year. The same species, run out to a Great Lake or the Pacific and back, becomes a steelhead. This guide covers identification, the water rainbows need, and how to catch them, from a family trip to a stocked pond to a chrome steelhead in March.

## How to identify a rainbow trout

A rainbow trout is a streamlined fish with small scales, a small fleshy adipose fin behind the dorsal fin, and a forked or squarish tail. The Missouri Department of Conservation and Wisconsin DNR list these marks:

- **Stripe:** a pink to red band along the side, faint on lake-run fish and bright on spawning males.
- **Spots:** black spots on the back, the top of the head, the dorsal and adipose fins, and all over the tail fin. MDC says there are "prominent dark spots on the tail."
- **No colored spots:** the sides lack orange or reddish spots.
- **Color:** back dark olive, steel-blue or green; sides silvery; belly white. The mouth is white inside.
- **Fins:** the anal fin usually has 10–11 rays (12 or fewer).

Steelhead and big lake rainbows are often almost pure silver when fresh, with the stripe showing only as they darken in rivers. NOAA's illustration describes a "silvery body, dark spots along its back, and faint pink side stripe."

### Rainbow vs brown vs brook trout

| Trait | Rainbow | Brown | Brook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Side | Pink or red stripe | Golden to brown | Dark olive with light wormy markings on the back |
| Spots | Black only | Black and red or orange, often with pale halos | Light spots, some red with blue halos |
| Tail | Heavily spotted | Few or no spots | Few spots, squarish |
| Lower fins | Plain | Plain or yellowish | White leading edge with a black stripe |

The tail is the quick test: spotted all over means rainbow. Some hatchery strains and hybrids, such as cutbows where cutthroat trout live, blur the lines.

## Range and habitat

Rainbows are native to the Pacific drainages of North America, from Alaska to Mexico and inland to the Rockies. According to the FAO, they have been introduced since 1874 to waters on every continent except Antarctica. In most of the eastern United States they are stocked, and MDC notes most Missouri populations are kept up by continuous stocking, with only a few self-sustaining streams.

Rainbows need cold, oxygen-rich water:

- Wisconsin DNR gives a preferred range of 53–57°F.
- MDC says trout water must stay "constantly below 70°F."
- The FAO lists a survival range of roughly 32–81°F, with growth and spawning best at about 48–57°F.

That cold-water need explains where you find them: spring creeks, mountain streams, deep cold lakes, and tailwaters below big dams that release cold water from the bottom of a reservoir. Lake Taneycomo in Missouri and the tailwaters of Middle Tennessee hold trout far south of any natural trout range for that reason.

In streams, look for trout where current brings food and a rock, log or depth change gives them a break: the seam between fast and slow water, the head and tail of a pool, the soft water behind a boulder, undercut banks. For small streams ("blue lines") a map gets you surprisingly far. As one angler on r/flyfishing put it, "satellite view on Google maps'll get you 80% there." [How to find fishing spots](/blog/how-to-find-fishing-spots) covers the rest, including public access.

## What rainbow trout eat

Aquatic insects, land insects, snails and small fish make up most of a rainbow's diet, per MDC. In practice that means mayfly, caddis and midge larvae drifting along the bottom most of the year, ants, beetles and hoppers falling in during summer, and fish eggs below spawning salmon and trout. Big lake and Great Lakes rainbows feed heavily on baitfish. Freshly stocked hatchery fish are used to pellets, so for their first days they hit almost anything that looks like food.

## Life cycle and spawning

- **Timing:** Most wild rainbows spawn once a year in spring, roughly January to May, according to the FAO. MDC says wild trout in Ozark springs spawn from late December through early February. Hatchery strains can be bred to spawn at other times, even year-round.
- **Temperature:** Growth and spawning happen best at about 48–57°F (9–14°C).
- **Nest:** The female digs a shallow pit, or redd, in gravelly riffles; one or more males fertilize the eggs, and there is no parental care.
- **Steelhead:** Great Lakes strains run at different times. Wisconsin DNR, for example, raises a Skamania strain that runs in July–August and spawns in January–February, a Chambers Creek strain that runs October–March and spawns in March, and a Ganaraska strain that runs in late March–April and spawns in late April.
- **Lifespan:** NOAA puts steelhead at up to 11 years, usually 5–8.

Leave spawning fish alone. In many trout streams the redds are easy to see as clean, light patches of gravel. Wading through them kills eggs, so step around.

## Seasonal patterns

| Season | Where rainbows are | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Winter | Slow, deep pools in streams; tailwaters; stocked ponds in the South | Small nymphs and midges, dough bait, slow presentations |
| Spring | Spread through runs and riffles as water warms; steelhead runs in Great Lakes rivers | Nymphs, spinners, worms; spawn sacs and beads for steelhead |
| Summer | Cold springs, shaded runs, deep lakes below the warm surface layer; tailwaters | Early and late fishing; terrestrial flies; trolled spoons in lakes |
| Fall | Feeding hard as water cools; steelhead start running in many rivers | Streamers, spinners, egg patterns |

In summer, if stream water is pushing 70°F, rainbows are stressed. Fish early in the morning or switch to a tailwater or a cold spring creek; see [water temperature and fish activity](/blog/water-temperature-and-fish-activity).

## How to catch rainbow trout

A 5'–6'6" ultralight spinning rod with 4–6 lb line handles stocked and stream trout. A 4–5 weight fly rod covers most streams, and steelhead call for a longer 7–8 weight or a 10–11 ft spinning or float rod with 8–12 lb line.

1. **Dough bait rig.** A 1/4–1/2 oz sliding sinker, a swivel, an 18–24 inch leader of 4 lb line and a #10–#12 treble or #8 single hook with floating dough bait, so it rides just off the bottom. The standard for stocked lakes and ponds.
2. **Worm or salmon eggs under a float.** A #8–#10 hook, a split shot and a small float, drifted through a run or set at mid-depth in a pond. (Check whether bait is allowed; many trout streams are artificial-only.)
3. **Inline spinner.** 1/16–1/8 oz, gold or silver blade, cast across and slightly upstream and reeled just fast enough to turn the blade.
4. **Small spoon.** 1/8–1/4 oz, for lakes and bigger rivers.
5. **Nymphs.** Size 14–18 bead-head nymphs under an indicator. In tailwaters, tiny midge patterns (size 18–22) often outfish everything.
6. **Steelhead.** Spawn sacs or 8–12 mm beads under a float, or a jig and wax worm, drifted through holes and tailouts.

Stocked fish crowd near the stocking point for a few days, then spread out. Most state agencies publish stocking schedules that tell you where and when. For a first trip with kids, a stocked pond and a dough bait rig is hard to beat; see [fishing for beginners](/blog/fishing-for-beginners).

<p class="callout warn"><strong>Tailwater safety:</strong> water below dams can rise several feet in minutes when generation starts. Check the dam operator's release schedule (TVA or the Corps of Engineers) before you wade, and get out at the first sign of rising water.</p>

## How big do rainbow trout get?

MDC lists rainbows at 10–15 inches and up to about 1½ lb, with hatchery fish reaching 10 inches in their first year. Wisconsin DNR lists Lake Michigan rainbows at 16–30 inches and 2–16 lb. NOAA puts steelhead at up to about 40 inches and 30 lb.

| Fish | Typical size | Big one |
|---|---|---|
| Stocked pond or stream rainbow | 9–12 in | 16 in+ holdover |
| Wild stream rainbow | 8–14 in | 20 in+ |
| Tailwater rainbow | 10–16 in | 20 in+, several pounds |
| Great Lakes steelhead | 16–30 in, 2–16 lb | 15 lb+ |
| World record | | 48 lb |

### The world record and how records are measured

Sean Konrad caught the IGFA all-tackle world record rainbow trout in Lake Diefenbaker, Saskatchewan, on September 5, 2009. It weighed 48 lb and measured 42 inches with a 32-inch girth.

IGFA all-tackle records are by weight, on land, on a scale certified within 12 months, with photos of the fish, the scale and the tackle; for fish of 25 lb or more, a new record must beat the old one by one-half of one percent. Separate length records exist for fish released alive. Wisconsin's Lake Michigan record, for comparison, is 24 lb 4 oz from 1973.

Trout are fragile out of water, so a quick photo matters more than a long weigh-in. Keep the fish wet, lay it alongside a measuring tape in the water or on a wet board, take one side-profile photo and let it go. ## Where to catch them in the pilot states

- **Tennessee:** Cold tailwaters below TVA and Corps dams, stocked largely from state and federal hatcheries; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Dale Hollow National Fish Hatchery alone planned to send out over 200,000 catchable trout in spring 2025, mostly to tailwaters in Middle Tennessee. See [Tennessee best lakes](/fishing/tennessee/best-lakes) and the [Tennessee species calendar](/fishing/tennessee/fish-species-seasons).
- **Michigan:** Inland trout streams and Great Lakes tributaries with spring and fall steelhead runs. See [Michigan best lakes](/fishing/michigan/best-lakes) and the [Michigan species calendar](/fishing/michigan/fish-species-seasons).

## Eating rainbow trout

Rainbow trout are mild and rich, and stocked fish in put-and-take waters are meant to be kept within the limits. Cook them whole or as fillets, skin on. Big Great Lakes steelhead and fish from some waters carry consumption advice, so check the EPA's [Choose Fish and Shellfish Wisely](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely) page for your state's advisory contacts.

## Regulations

Trout rules are the most varied in fresh water. Some states require a separate trout stamp or endorsement, and streams can be bait-allowed, artificial-only, fly-only or catch-and-release, with different seasons and size limits on neighboring stretches. Several Pacific steelhead populations are protected under the Endangered Species Act, which brings strict rules on handling and keeping wild fish. Check the exact stretch of water on your state agency's site before you go, starting with your state's page, for example [Tennessee fishing license](/fishing/tennessee/fishing-license) or [Michigan fishing license](/fishing/michigan/fishing-license).
